Continuous Track

A tracked vehicle lays its own road, drives along it and picks it up again. Drive one over a proving ground and change almost everything about it.

How it works ▾

  • The link on the ground stands still. Watch the orange link: it is laid down at the front, lies still while every road wheel rolls over it, and is lifted at the back. The top of the belt therefore runs at twice the vehicle's speed.
  • The sprocket pulls belt, the ground pushes the vehicle. Teeth drag the belt round. The pads shear the soil backwards, and the soil's reaction is the thrust. Thrust needs some slip; the thrust-slip chart shows how much.
  • Low ground pressure. The weight is spread over two long strips. On soft ground that means less sinkage, and cohesive soil adds grip in proportion to the area (c·A). On concrete, tracks grip no better than tyres.
  • Steering is a speed difference. A track cannot point anywhere but straight, so in a turn the front of each track is dragged sideways one way and the rear the other. Turn on the traction arrows and pivot on the spot to see it. It costs a lot of power.
  • Where the power goes in a turn depends on the steering gear: clutch-and-brake wastes the inner track's energy as heat and cannot turn on the spot; a double differential hands it to the outer track.
  • Obstacles. The sloping run of track between the first road wheel and the raised front wheel is what climbs a step. The length of track on the ground is what bridges a trench.

What is simplified ▾

  • The belt follows a computed path. Links are not separate bodies, so a track cannot be thrown or break. Tension is estimated from sprocket force and pretension, not solved.
  • Thrust under each wheel station follows a shear-displacement law limited by c·A + N·tan φ. Sinkage follows Bekker's pressure-sinkage law per station. No bulldozing in front of the track, no effect of earlier passes.
  • Soil numbers are the usual textbook values (after Wong), typed from memory and not re-checked. Concrete grip assumes rubber pads.
  • Road wheels move straight up and down and have no mass of their own. The ground is a height field: a wheel cannot hook under an overhang.
  • Step climbing is optimistic. The belt grips a vertical concrete face as well as it grips level ground, so the default tank gets up about 1.6 m where a real one manages about 1.1 m.
  • The engine is a power limit with an ideal transmission and a speed governor; the lever position sets a target speed.
  • The tyre comparison assumes contact pressure equals inflation pressure and two ruts.
