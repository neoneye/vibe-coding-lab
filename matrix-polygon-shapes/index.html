Investigation · matrix-polygon-shapes

Can square matrices be replaced by n-gons?

A matrix is a rectangle of numbers. What if the numbers were laid out as a triangle, a pentagon or a hexagon? "Multiplying shapes" can mean three precise things, and each gives a different, sometimes surprising answer.

Shape = nonzero pattern of a matrix
Up to 5 corners

Rectangles, triangles, trapezoids and certain pentagons survive multiplication. Hexagons and larger shapes cannot stay stable: products square them up.

Shape = array, multiplied by convolution
Any polygon works

Shapes add (Minkowski sum). Cost is exactly 4|P| − B − 3. Triangles and hexagons are as natural as squares. Without wrap-around the centre of a hexagon learns 30× faster than its corners.

Shape = ring of matrices
Odd beats even

Even rings (square, hexagon) admit no shortcut: rank 2^k. Odd rings (triangle, pentagon) do. The pentagon's exact cost (25 to 31) is an open problem.

Shaped weights in a language model
Close, not better

Triangles and cubes of weights beat a plain MLP but not SwiGLU. A whole hexagon-pyramid model with triangle layers comes close to a standard transformer: the best version (wrapped) is 0.013 bits/char behind at 4,000 steps, within seed noise.

The common thread: the rectangle is what multiplication converges to, and other shapes earn their place only with a different multiplication. See Takeaways.

1 · Shapes as nonzero patterns inside a matrix

Keep ordinary matrix multiplication, but allow nonzeros only inside a shape drawn on the N×N grid. The product of two such matrices has nonzeros where (x,z) can be reached through some middle index y with both (x,y) and (y,z) inside the shape. That is the relational composition R∘R. A shape is useful as a "matrix type" only if it is closed: the product of two such matrices has the same shape.

Boolean products on a 64×64 grid, computed live. Cost counts Σy |column y| · |row y| scalar multiplications for a sparse product, relative to the dense N³.

What survives multiplication

  • Stable shapes (R∘R = R) have at most 5 corners. Any box whose row and column ranges overlap is stable, and stays stable when cut by the diagonal half-plane x ≤ y (or x ≥ y). That gives rectangles, triangles, trapezoids and pentagons, and that direction is proved. In a random search of 4,171 polygons, all 507 stable ones had only horizontal, vertical and diagonal edges. So no hexagon or larger n-gon is stable (numerical evidence for this direction).
  • Closed but dying. A shape whose column range misses its row range multiplies to zero, and some shapes shrink into themselves. Those can be any n-gon, but their powers vanish (nilpotent).
  • Multiplication squares things up. Under repeated products, every shape crossing the diagonal converges to its bounding rectangle. Band matrices, which are hexagons inside the square, fill the whole matrix after about 1/bandwidth products.
  • The triangle is the classic win: closed, the building block of LU and Cholesky factorisations, and a product costs N³/6 instead of N³. But upper × lower is dense.
  • A triangle is also how symmetric matrices are stored. Their ordinary product isn't symmetric, but the Jordan product (AB + BA)/2 and powers Aᵏ are. So triangle storage is closed under those, even though the triangle pattern of a symmetric matrix is the whole square.

2 · Shaped arrays multiplied by convolution

A non-rectangular array has no natural "row times column" product. It does have a natural product, though: treat it as a two-variable polynomial (or an image filter) and convolve. The shape of the result is the Minkowski sum P + Q, so a pentagon times a pentagon is a pentagon twice the size. The exact minimum number of multiplications is the number of lattice points in P + Q. By Pick's theorem that is 4|P| − B − 3, where B counts the lattice points on the boundary.

Blue: the support P of one factor. Orange: the support of the product, P + P. Each orange point is one output coefficient, so one unavoidable multiplication.
Two arrays of the same shape, about 100 coefficients each. "Exact" is the proven minimum |P+P|, which equals 4|P| − B − 3 in every row.
  • Exact cost. The output coefficients are independent, so at least |P+Q| multiplications are needed. Evaluating both arrays at |P+Q| generic points, multiplying, and interpolating attains it. The catch is numerical: that interpolation is badly conditioned (condition numbers of 10⁶–10¹⁰ already for 15–25 points), which is why practice uses FFTs on boxes or hexagons.
  • Boundary points are cheap. Lattice-aligned edges (triangle, square, hexagon) carry many lattice points. Pentagon, octagon and disk edges cut the lattice at awkward angles and carry few, so at equal size they cost about 4% more per coefficient.
  • No gaps in 2D, gaps in 3D. For every 2D lattice polygon, (P∩Z²) + (P∩Z²) = (2P)∩Z² (0 failures in 300 random polygons). In 3D this fails: the double of the Reeve tetrahedron contains (1,1,1) and (1,1,2), which are not sums of two of its points.
  • Regular polygons exist on a lattice only for n = 3, 4, 6 (the crystallographic restriction). Pentagons and octagons can only be approximated, which is why their boundaries are lattice-poor.
  • Mixing shapes: triangle + reflected triangle = hexagon (correlating triangular arrays yields hexagons); square + diamond = octagon; pentagon + reflected pentagon = decagon.

3 · A polygon of matrices: trace(A₁A₂···Ak)

Put a matrix on each edge of a k-gon and multiply around the ring. This k-linear form is the k-gon tensor network. The triangle is ordinary matrix multiplication, trace(ABC), with Strassen's 7 products for 2×2. The square is the four-way form trace(ABCD) from the three-way project. Its rank N⁴ means no fused algorithm beats the naive one. The tensor rank is the number of products a fused algorithm needs. For 2×2 matrices (bond dimension 2):

The button builds Christandl–Zuiddam's decompositions in your browser from Strassen's: 31 products for the pentagon, 127 for the heptagon. It then checks them entry by entry against the ring tensor.
  • Even polygons give no shortcut. Splitting the ring into alternating vertices cuts every edge, and that flattening has rank 2^k, equal to the number of naive terms. So square, hexagon and octagon rings have rank exactly 2^k.
  • Odd polygons do, because the alternating split must leave one edge uncut. Christandl–Zuiddam's tensor surgery turns Strassen's triangle into 2^k − 1 products for every odd k: 31 for the pentagon, 127 for the heptagon.
  • The pentagon is open: 25 ≤ R ≤ 31. The lower bound comes from a Young flattening (Buhrman et al.), which also bounds the border rank. We attacked it numerically:
    • random restarts, and restarts from the exact 31-term decomposition with one term removed, all stall at error exactly 1;
    • a border-rank probe at 30 terms from there shows no divergence;
    • an exact GF(2) SAT search is described below;
    • a rotation-symmetric search (orbits of 5 rotated terms plus fixed terms, the form in which it finds Strassen's triangle) gives one lead. At 30 = 5·5 + 5 terms the error keeps falling, 0.044 → 0.025 over a long run, while the coefficients slowly grow (error ∝ norm−2.1). That is the signature of an approximate (border) decomposition, which would beat the best known border bound of 31. It is not confirmed: double precision runs out long before the error could reach zero. The 29- and 31-term symmetric fits freeze at 0.041 and 0.0003.
    So the exact rank stays open. 31 has not been beaten exactly, and there is one unconfirmed numerical hint that 30 terms might work approximately.

4 · Triangle-shaped weights, swept three ways

Store weights on a triangular lattice: cells (i, j, k) with i + j + k = n − 1, about n²/2 of them. Each cell lies on three lines, at 0°, 60° and 120°. A matrix entry lies on only two: its row and its column. Sweeping each direction multiplies the two other coordinates' inputs and sums along the line. So one set of weights gives three outputs:

y₀[i] = Σ W·hj·hk   y₁[j] = Σ W·hi·hk   y₂[k] = Σ W·hi·hj

Together the three sweeps are exactly the gradient of the cubic energy E(h) = Σ Wijk·hi·hj·hk. That makes it a multiplicative layer, in the same family as the gated layers (GLU, SwiGLU) that modern language models use.

Click any cell (n = 8, 36 weights). Blue: the 0° line (output y₀[i]). Orange: the 60° line (y₁[j]). Green: the 120° line (y₂[k]). The check computes all three sweeps and compares their sum with a finite-difference gradient of E.

Does it help a language model?

A 2-layer, width-64 character-level transformer was trained on this repository's markdown (2.8 MB, 1,500 steps, CPU). Only the feed-forward block differed, and every variant had about 65.5k feed-forward weights. Lower is better:

Validation bits per character after 1,500 steps. Dots are individual seeds (two each, one for the one-sweep triangle). Script: llm/triangle_ffn.py.
  • It works: both triangle layers beat the standard MLP at the same number of weights.
  • It isn't the best: the standard gated layer, SwiGLU, wins.
  • Three sweeps don't help quality per weight. Each extra direction needs its own output weights, so at a fixed budget the three-sweep triangle must be smaller (n = 106 against 158 for one sweep).
  • What three sweeps buy is reuse: each triangle weight feeds 6 multiplications per read, against 1 for an ordinary matrix weight. Generating text one token at a time is limited by memory bandwidth, so that is where this could pay off. Testing it needs a fused GPU kernel; this CPU version runs about 25× slower per step than the MLP, an implementation artefact.
  • Triangles already work elsewhere in big models, over tokens or pairs rather than weights. AlphaFold's triangle updates refresh each residue pair from the other two edges of every triangle containing it, swept in two directions. 2-simplicial attention (Meta, 2025) attends over triangles of tokens and improves token efficiency on maths, code and reasoning.
  • Caveat: tiny model, short run, CPU. This is a smoke test, not a scaling result.

In 3D: a cube and a tetrahedron

A cube of weights W[i, j, k] is a full 3-way array. Each weight sits on 3 lines, one per axis, and the triangle above is just its diagonal slice i + j + k = n − 1. A tetrahedron (cells with i + j + k + l = n − 1) has 4 face directions. Each sweep collapses a triangular slice and multiplies the other three inputs, giving a 4th-order (quartic) interaction. In general a d-dimensional simplex has d + 1 sweeps and d(d + 1) multiplications per weight read.

Counted by running each shape's sweep code (n = 5), which also checks that the sweeps add up to the gradient of the shape's energy.

  • Rounding everything to the same bit width gives no bonus. At 3–4 bits the triangle and cube degrade slightly faster than the MLP and the gated block.

5 · Hexagons: wrap around or not, and hexagonal attention

Multiply two hexagonal arrays by convolution. With wrap-around (hexagons tiling a torus) every output cell receives exactly the same number of multiplications, |P|, just like an ordinary cyclic product. Without wrap-around the count is the overlap of two hexagons: |P| at the centre, falling to 1 at the corners.

Darker means more multiplications land on that output cell. Wrapped, the doubled hexagon folds back onto the original one and every cell gets the same count.

Does the centre learn faster?

Yes. Under gradient descent an output's learning speed is its neural-tangent-kernel diagonal, Σp b²s−p + a²p, which grows with the number of products landing on it. Both factors were trained (y = a ∗ b, random targets, 12 seeds, hexagons of radius 4):

Average error removed per output in the first 3 gradient steps, by ring (0 = centre). Orange: no wrap-around. Blue: wrap-around (only rings 0–4 exist after folding).
  • Without wrap-around the centre learns about 30× faster than the corners (learning speed 41 against 2; error removed 9.4 against 0.29). The edges are under-trained, a built-in curriculum from the inside out.
  • With wrap-around every output learns at the same speed, like an ordinary dense or cyclic product.
  • The input cells, by contrast, are uniform either way: each one meets |P| outputs. Only the outputs differ.

A hexagonal attention or convolution mask

The idea: let each cell attend strongly to its 6 neighbours, skip the next ring, and attend to a few random far cells. That is a hexagonal cousin of BigBird's local + random sparse attention.

Hexagonal map of radius 8 (217 cells). Dark: the centre cell. Blue: what it attends to directly. Shaded: what has reached it after the chosen number of layers. The table gives links per cell (cost) and layers until every cell has heard from every other.
  • Random far links are cheap and powerful. Ring 1 alone needs 16 layers to connect a radius-8 map. Adding 3 random far cells per cell needs only 5, with 9.5 links per cell against 16.7 for a dense two-ring mask that still needs 8. It's the small-world effect.
  • Skipping ring 2 works like a dilated kernel: ring 1 + ring 3 connects the map in 6 layers.
  • Hexagonal blurs are rounder. Repeating a 7-cell hexagonal blur gives a spread that is exactly isotropic in its 4th moments (max/min over directions 1.000 at every step). Square blurs are only isotropic in their 2nd moments: their 4th-moment ratio is 1.29 after 2 steps (4 neighbours) and still 1.04 after 10. Six-fold symmetry cancels the 4-fold term that squares keep.
Max/min over directions of the 4th moment E[(x·u)⁴] after repeated blurring, computed live. 1.000 means perfectly round up to 4th order.

In a language model: does the non-uniformity help?

Arrange the hidden units of the feed-forward block on a hexagon and let the block convolve two projections of the input across it: y = C·((A·x) ∗ (B·x)). Without wrap-around the output hexagon has twice the radius and is centre-heavy; with wrap-around every output gets the same number of products. This is computed exactly with an FFT, because a hexagon of radius R on a torus is the cyclic group ZN, N = 3R² + 3R + 1, via φ(q, r) = ((R+1)·q − R·r) mod N. The question is not whether it saves operations, but whether the shape gives the model a property it can use.

  • The centre-heavy layer learns an importance order on its own. Trimming the outer rings hurts 2–3× less than dropping random cells (82% of cells kept: +0.08 against +0.23). The uniform, wrapped layer has no such order: outer and random drops cost the same. That gives a ready-made dial for trimming compute at inference, outer rings first. It isn't free (trimming a third costs +0.23), but it's much gentler than trimming at random.
  • More precision at the centre does not help. With the output weights at a 3-bit average: uniform +0.18, more bits at the centre +0.34, more bits at the edge +0.49. Squeezing any ring down to 2 bits costs more than the others gain.
  • Training pushes back against the non-uniformity. Edge activations are about 5× weaker than the centre's, so the edge output weights grow larger and move about 70% further during training. The compensation is partial: the centre still contributes about 3× more, which is why the outer rings are cheaper to trim.
  • Next: train with random outer-ring dropout (Matryoshka-style) to sharpen the order, and use it for elastic width or early exit.

Where this matters: hexagonal grids are standard in geospatial indexing (Uber's H3), board and strategy game maps, telescope and detector arrays, and hexagonal CNNs (HexagDLy). A hexagonal local + skip + random mask is a natural sparse attention for such data. Choosing wrap-around or not decides whether the border learns as fast as the middle.

Takeaways

The common thread: the rectangle is the attractor. A matrix is a rectangle because a linear map pairs every input with every output. Shapes drawn inside that rectangle drift back towards rectangles when you multiply. Every centred shape becomes its bounding box within 2–3 products. Other shapes stay themselves only when the multiplication changes. Convolution adds shapes instead of composing them, and keeps every convex polygon. And when the polygon describes how matrices are wired together (a ring), what matters is not the shape but its parity.

Surprises

  1. A pentagon can be a stable matrix shape, a hexagon cannot. The more symmetric shape loses. A stable shape is a box cut by at most one diagonal line, and a rectangle cut by one line has at most 5 corners.
  2. Geometry gives the exact cost. Multiplying two P-shaped arrays needs exactly 4|P| − B − 3 multiplications, by Pick's theorem. So the number of lattice points on the edge sets the cost. That's why pentagons, octagons and disks, which can't be regular on a lattice, are about 4% more expensive. The crystallographic restriction shows up as a price.
  3. Odd versus even decides everything for rings. Square, hexagon and octagon rings are incompressible: every fused algorithm needs all 2^k terms. Triangle, pentagon and heptagon rings are compressible. Strassen's speed-up is, in this light, an odd-cycle phenomenon.
  4. Hexagons are made of triangles: a triangular array correlated with its mirror image gives a hexagonal array.
  5. 2D is special. A doubled lattice polygon never has gaps in 2D, but a doubled tetrahedron can (Reeve), so 3D shaped arrays are less tidy.
  6. The natural symmetric generalisation of Strassen doesn't simply appear for the pentagon. A rotation-symmetric 31-term fit froze at error 0.0003. The only lead below 31 is an unconfirmed 30-term approximate decomposition.
  7. The three-way weights tolerate coarse rounding, the projections don't. The cube's 49k three-way weights at 2 bits hurt less than one 33k MLP matrix at 2 bits. The sensitive part is the input projection, whose errors are multiplied together.
  8. Without wrap-around, a hexagon's centre learns about 30× faster than its corners, because more products land there. Wrap-around makes learning uniform.
  9. Hexagonal blurs are exactly round up to 4th order at every step; square blurs only become round slowly.
  10. In a whole model, the triangle beats the hexagon by a wide margin. A pyramid built on hexagonal feature cells with 3 → 1 bottlenecks came close to a standard transformer once its feed-forward was the triangle layer (the wrapped triangle is 0.013 bits/char behind at 4,000 steps, within seed noise), and stayed 0.2 behind with the hexagon's local mixing. The pyramid was never the problem.
  11. The "centre" means different things in the two shapes. In the triangle the long lines are signal (lowering them hurts, raising them is free); in the hexagon the centre-heavy sums were neither harmful nor needed (1/√ balancing changes nothing).
  12. A centre-heavy hexagonal layer learns an importance order without being asked. In a language model, trimming its outer rings hurts 2–3× less than trimming at random. Meanwhile training partly fights the non-uniformity, growing the edge weights to make up for weaker edge activations.
  13. Exact search hits a wall quickly. A SAT solver settles the triangle instantly. But it couldn't prove within 15 minutes that the square ring needs more than 15 products, a fact that a one-line flattening argument proves.

Where this is useful

  • Attention masks are shapes. A causal mask is a triangle, which is closed, so stacking causal layers stays causal. A sliding-window causal mask is a band cut by the triangle. It is not closed: each product widens the band. That widening is exactly how a stack of sliding-window layers gets a receptive field of about (layers × window). Pick these two masks in section 1 to see it.
  • Storage formats that stay closed: triangles, trapezoids and the triangle-in-a-window pentagon can be multiplied without the result leaving the format. Bands and centred shapes need re-allocation after every product.
  • Choosing an array shape for convolution data: hexagons for round, isotropic data (images, sensors, game maps; 13.4% fewer samples); triangles for polynomials of bounded total degree. The cost formula 4|P| − B − 3 lets you budget the work exactly.
  • Mixed-precision storage of shaped layers: keep the small input projection precise and store the three-way weights at 3 bits. In the tiny model the cube then beat the gated block at the same storage (3.530 vs 3.602 bits/char at about 200k bits).
  • Hexagonal data (geospatial H3 cells, game maps, detector arrays): a ring-1 + skip + random sparse mask connects a radius-8 map in 5 layers at 9.5 links per cell, against 16 layers for neighbours only. Wrap-around or not is a design choice: uniform learning, or a centre-first curriculum.
  • Elastic width for free-ish: a non-wrapped hexagonal feed-forward layer orders its own outputs by importance, so outer rings can be trimmed at inference when compute is short, at a fraction of the cost of trimming at random.
  • Tensor networks with loops: an even loop can't be shortcut by any fused algorithm, so the order of pairwise contractions is all that matters. Odd loops leave room for Strassen-like tricks.
  • Shaped weights in a language model (triangle, cube; the tetrahedron was too slow to judge) train fine and beat a plain MLP, but not SwiGLU. The cube did best. Their selling point would be 6 multiplications per weight read for memory-bound inference, which is untested without a GPU kernel. The triangles that already pay off in large models live over tokens (2-simplicial attention) or residue pairs (AlphaFold).

How new is this?

Most of it is known in some field: triangular matrices, receptive fields, Newton polytopes and Minkowski sums, Pick's theorem, hexagonal sampling, and Christandl–Zuiddam's cycle tensors. What this page adds is the side-by-side view, the classification of stable matrix shapes (at most 5 corners), the closed cost formula 4|P| − B − 3 for shaped convolution, and the unconfirmed hint that the pentagon ring might be approximated with 30 terms. Also new are the small experiments: shaped weight layers swept three or four ways; the finding that their three-way weights are robust to low precision while the input projection is not; the hexagonal wrap / no-wrap learning-speed contrast; and the emergent importance order of a centre-heavy hexagonal layer. The isotropy of hexagonal blurs and the power of random long-range links (BigBird, small-world graphs) are known.

Pros and cons by shape

shapeas a matrix patternas a convolution arrayas a ring of matrices
Triangle + closed, N³/6 per product, the basis of LU/Cholesky and triangular solves. − upper × lower is dense; only half the linear maps. + natural for total-degree polynomials (Taylor series), lattice-regular, cheapest per coefficient at equal size (rank/|P| 3.60). − an FFT on a box wastes about half the space. + this is ordinary matrix multiplication; Strassen's 7. − the exact rank for N ≥ 3 is a famous open problem.
As weights swept three ways: + 6× weight reuse; beats an MLP. − behind SwiGLU; three sweeps no better than one per weight.
Square + all linear maps; closed. − nothing to exploit. + separable, FFT-native, all the tooling. − anisotropic: corners waste coefficients for round problems. − rank exactly 2^k (N⁴ in general): no fused shortcut.
Pentagon + the special pentagon "upper triangle ∩ row/column window" is closed. − regular or centred pentagons spread into rectangles. + P + P is again a pentagon, with no gaps. − cannot be regular on a lattice; few boundary points make it about 4% more expensive; no FFT. + odd, so it has shortcuts: 31 instead of 32. − the true optimum (25 to 31) is unknown.
Hexagon − band matrices are hexagons and grow with every product; never stable. + regular on the hex lattice, isotropic, needs 13.4% fewer samples for round band-limited signals; hex FFTs exist; triangle ⊕ reflected triangle. − less tooling than squares. − even ring: rank exactly 2^k, no shortcut.
Octagon, n-gon, disk − centred shapes become rectangles after 1–2 products. + closest to isotropic. − not lattice-regular; fewest boundary points, so most expensive per coefficient. odd n: shortcuts of 2^n − 1 or better; even n: none.

So can square matrices be replaced by n-gons?

  • As general linear maps, no. A linear map pairs every input with every output, and that table of pairs is a rectangle. Any other shape is a restriction of it. Only restrictions with at most 5 corners stay closed under multiplication, and the useful one is the triangle, which numerical linear algebra already exploits everywhere.
  • As data with their own multiplication, yes. For convolution-type products (polynomials, filters, images, hex grids), triangles and hexagons are as natural as squares: the product keeps the shape, and the cost is exactly 4|P| − B − 3. Hexagons are the better choice for round, isotropic data; triangles for polynomials of bounded total degree.
  • As the multiplication pattern, odd polygons are where the shortcuts live. Every even ring is incompressible. Every odd ring inherits a speed-up from Strassen's triangle, and the pentagon's exact cost is still open.
