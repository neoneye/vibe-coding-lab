Investigation · matrix-polygon-shapes

Can square matrices be replaced by n-gons?

A matrix is a rectangle of numbers. What if the numbers were laid out as a triangle, a pentagon or a hexagon? "Multiplying shapes" can mean three precise things, and each gives a different, sometimes surprising answer.

Shape = nonzero pattern of a matrix
Up to 5 corners

Rectangles, triangles, trapezoids and certain pentagons survive multiplication. Hexagons and larger shapes cannot stay stable: products square them up.

Shape = array, multiplied by convolution
Any polygon works

Shapes add (Minkowski sum). Cost is exactly 4|P| − B − 3. Triangles and hexagons are as natural as squares. Without wrap-around the centre of a hexagon learns 30× faster than its corners.

Shape = ring of matrices
Odd beats even

Even rings (square, hexagon) admit no shortcut: rank 2^k. Odd rings (triangle, pentagon) do. The pentagon's exact cost (25 to 31) is an open problem.

Shaped weights in a language model
Close, not better

Triangles and cubes of weights beat a plain MLP but not SwiGLU. A whole hexagon-pyramid model with triangle layers comes close to a standard transformer: the best version (wrapped) is 0.013 bits/char behind at 4,000 steps, within seed noise.

The common thread: the rectangle is what multiplication converges to, and other shapes earn their place only with a different multiplication. See Takeaways.

1 · Shapes as nonzero patterns inside a matrix

Keep ordinary matrix multiplication, but allow nonzeros only inside a shape drawn on the N×N grid. The product of two such matrices has nonzeros where (x,z) can be reached through some middle index y with both (x,y) and (y,z) inside the shape. That is the relational composition R∘R. A shape is useful as a "matrix type" only if it is closed: the product of two such matrices has the same shape.

Boolean products on a 64×64 grid, computed live. Cost counts Σy |column y| · |row y| scalar multiplications for a sparse product, relative to the dense N³.

What survives multiplication

  • Stable shapes (R∘R = R) have at most 5 corners. Any box whose row and column ranges overlap is stable, and stays stable when cut by the diagonal half-plane x ≤ y (or x ≥ y). That gives rectangles, triangles, trapezoids and pentagons, and that direction is proved. In a random search of 4,171 polygons, all 507 stable ones had only horizontal, vertical and diagonal edges. So no hexagon or larger n-gon is stable (numerical evidence for this direction).
  • Closed but dying. A shape whose column range misses its row range multiplies to zero, and some shapes shrink into themselves. Those can be any n-gon, but their powers vanish (nilpotent).
  • Multiplication squares things up. Under repeated products, every shape crossing the diagonal converges to its bounding rectangle. Band matrices, which are hexagons inside the square, fill the whole matrix after about 1/bandwidth products.
  • The triangle is the classic win: closed, the building block of LU and Cholesky factorisations, and a product costs N³/6 instead of N³. But upper × lower is dense.
  • A triangle is also how symmetric matrices are stored. Their ordinary product isn't symmetric, but the Jordan product (AB + BA)/2 and powers Aᵏ are. So triangle storage is closed under those, even though the triangle pattern of a symmetric matrix is the whole square.

2 · Shaped arrays multiplied by convolution

A non-rectangular array has no natural "row times column" product. It does have a natural product, though: treat it as a two-variable polynomial (or an image filter) and convolve. The shape of the result is the Minkowski sum P + Q, so a pentagon times a pentagon is a pentagon twice the size. The exact minimum number of multiplications is the number of lattice points in P + Q. By Pick's theorem that is 4|P| − B − 3, where B counts the lattice points on the boundary.

Blue: the support P of one factor. Orange: the support of the product, P + P. Each orange point is one output coefficient, so one unavoidable multiplication.
Two arrays of the same shape, about 100 coefficients each. "Exact" is the proven minimum |P+P|, which equals 4|P| − B − 3 in every row.
  • Exact cost. The output coefficients are independent, so at least |P+Q| multiplications are needed. Evaluating both arrays at |P+Q| generic points, multiplying, and interpolating attains it. The catch is numerical: that interpolation is badly conditioned (condition numbers of 10⁶–10¹⁰ already for 15–25 points), which is why practice uses FFTs on boxes or hexagons.
  • Boundary points are cheap. Lattice-aligned edges (triangle, square, hexagon) carry many lattice points. Pentagon, octagon and disk edges cut the lattice at awkward angles and carry few, so at equal size they cost about 4% more per coefficient.
  • No gaps in 2D, gaps in 3D. For every 2D lattice polygon, (P∩Z²) + (P∩Z²) = (2P)∩Z² (0 failures in 300 random polygons). In 3D this fails: the double of the Reeve tetrahedron contains (1,1,1) and (1,1,2), which are not sums of two of its points.
  • Regular polygons exist on a lattice only for n = 3, 4, 6 (the crystallographic restriction). Pentagons and octagons can only be approximated, which is why their boundaries are lattice-poor.
  • Mixing shapes: triangle + reflected triangle = hexagon (correlating triangular arrays yields hexagons); square + diamond = octagon; pentagon + reflected pentagon = decagon.

3 · A polygon of matrices: trace(A₁A₂···Ak)

Put a matrix on each edge of a k-gon and multiply around the ring. This k-linear form is the k-gon tensor network. The triangle is ordinary matrix multiplication, trace(ABC), with Strassen's 7 products for 2×2. The square is the four-way form trace(ABCD) from the three-way project. Its rank N⁴ means no fused algorithm beats the naive one. The tensor rank is the number of products a fused algorithm needs. For 2×2 matrices (bond dimension 2):

The button builds Christandl–Zuiddam's decompositions in your browser from Strassen's: 31 products for the pentagon, 127 for the heptagon. It then checks them entry by entry against the ring tensor.
  • Even polygons give no shortcut. Splitting the ring into alternating vertices cuts every edge, and that flattening has rank 2^k, equal to the number of naive terms. So square, hexagon and octagon rings have rank exactly 2^k.
  • Odd polygons do, because the alternating split must leave one edge uncut. Christandl–Zuiddam's tensor surgery turns Strassen's triangle into 2^k − 1 products for every odd k: 31 for the pentagon, 127 for the heptagon.
  • The pentagon is open: 25 ≤ R ≤ 31. The lower bound comes from a Young flattening (Buhrman et al.), which also bounds the border rank. We attacked it numerically:
    • random restarts, and restarts from the exact 31-term decomposition with one term removed, all stall at error exactly 1;
    • a border-rank probe at 30 terms from there shows no divergence;
    • an exact GF(2) SAT search is described below;
    • a rotation-symmetric search (orbits of 5 rotated terms plus fixed terms, the form in which it finds Strassen's triangle) gives one lead. At 30 = 5·5 + 5 terms the error keeps falling, 0.044 → 0.025 over a long run, while the coefficients slowly grow (error ∝ norm−2.1). That is the signature of an approximate (border) decomposition, which would beat the best known border bound of 31. It is not confirmed: double precision runs out long before the error could reach zero. The 29- and 31-term symmetric fits freeze at 0.041 and 0.0003.
    So the exact rank stays open. 31 has not been beaten exactly, and there is one unconfirmed numerical hint that 30 terms might work approximately.

4 · Triangle-shaped weights, swept three ways

Store weights on a triangular lattice: cells (i, j, k) with i + j + k = n − 1, about n²/2 of them. Each cell lies on three lines, at 0°, 60° and 120°. A matrix entry lies on only two: its row and its column. Sweeping each direction multiplies the two other coordinates' inputs and sums along the line. So one set of weights gives three outputs:

y₀[i] = Σ W·hj·hk   y₁[j] = Σ W·hi·hk   y₂[k] = Σ W·hi·hj

Together the three sweeps are exactly the gradient of the cubic energy E(h) = Σ Wijk·hi·hj·hk. That makes it a multiplicative layer, in the same family as the gated layers (GLU, SwiGLU) that modern language models use.

Does it help a language model?

A 2-layer, width-64 character-level transformer was trained on this repository's markdown (2.8 MB, 1,500 steps, CPU). Only the feed-forward block differed, and every variant had about 65.5k feed-forward weights. Lower is better:

Validation bits per character after 1,500 steps. Dots are individual seeds (two each, one for the one-sweep triangle). Script: llm/triangle_ffn.py.
  • It works: both triangle layers beat the standard MLP at the same number of weights.
  • It isn't the best: the standard gated layer, SwiGLU, wins.
  • Three sweeps don't help quality per weight. Each extra direction needs its own output weights, so at a fixed budget the three-sweep triangle must be smaller (n = 106 against 158 for one sweep).
  • What three sweeps buy is reuse: each triangle weight feeds 6 multiplications per read, against 1 for an ordinary matrix weight. Generating text one token at a time is limited by memory bandwidth, so that is where this could pay off. Testing it needs a fused GPU kernel; this CPU version runs about 25× slower per step than the MLP, an implementation artefact.
  • Triangles already work elsewhere in big models, over tokens or pairs rather than weights. AlphaFold's triangle updates refresh each residue pair from the other two edges of every triangle containing it, swept in two directions. 2-simplicial attention (Meta, 2025) attends over triangles of tokens and improves token efficiency on maths, code and reasoning.
  • Caveat: tiny model, short run, CPU. This is a smoke test, not a scaling result.

In 3D: a cube and a tetrahedron

A cube of weights W[i, j, k] is a full 3-way array. Each weight sits on 3 lines, one per axis, and the triangle above is just its diagonal slice i + j + k = n − 1. A tetrahedron (cells with i + j + k + l = n − 1) has 4 face directions. Each sweep collapses a triangular slice and multiplies the other three inputs, giving a 4th-order (quartic) interaction. In general a d-dimensional simplex has d + 1 sweeps and d(d + 1) multiplications per weight read.

Counted by running each shape's sweep code (n = 5), which also checks that the sweeps add up to the gradient of the shape's energy.

5 · Hexagons: wrap around or not, and hexagonal attention

Multiply two hexagonal arrays by convolution. With wrap-around (hexagons tiling a torus) every output cell receives exactly the same number of multiplications, |P|, just like an ordinary cyclic product. Without wrap-around the count is the overlap of two hexagons: |P| at the centre, falling to 1 at the corners.

Darker means more multiplications land on that output cell. Wrapped, the doubled hexagon folds back onto the original one and every cell gets the same count.

Does the centre learn faster?

Yes. Under gradient descent an output's learning speed is its neural-tangent-kernel diagonal, Σp b²s−p + a²p, which grows with the number of products landing on it. Both factors were trained (y = a ∗ b, random targets, 12 seeds, hexagons of radius 4):

Average error removed per output in the first 3 gradient steps, by ring (0 = centre). Orange: no wrap-around. Blue: wrap-around (only rings 0–4 exist after folding).
  • Without wrap-around the centre learns about 30× faster than the corners (learning speed 41 against 2; error removed 9.4 against 0.29). The edges are under-trained, a built-in curriculum from the inside out.
  • With wrap-around every output learns at the same speed, like an ordinary dense or cyclic product.
  • The input cells, by contrast, are uniform either way: each one meets |P| outputs. Only the outputs differ.

A hexagonal attention or convolution mask

The idea: let each cell attend strongly to its 6 neighbours, skip the next ring, and attend to a few random far cells. That is a hexagonal cousin of BigBird's local + random sparse attention.

Hexagonal map of radius 8 (217 cells). Dark: the centre cell. Blue: what it attends to directly. Shaded: what has reached it after the chosen number of layers. The table gives links per cell (cost) and layers until every cell has heard from every other.
  • Random far links are cheap and powerful. Ring 1 alone needs 16 layers to connect a radius-8 map. Adding 3 random far cells per cell needs only 5, with 9.5 links per cell against 16.7 for a dense two-ring mask that still needs 8. It's the small-world effect.
  • Skipping ring 2 works like a dilated kernel: ring 1 + ring 3 connects the map in 6 layers.
  • Hexagonal blurs are rounder. Repeating a 7-cell hexagonal blur gives a spread that is exactly isotropic in its 4th moments (max/min over directions 1.000 at every step). Square blurs are only isotropic in their 2nd moments: their 4th-moment ratio is 1.29 after 2 steps (4 neighbours) and still 1.04 after 10. Six-fold symmetry cancels the 4-fold term that squares keep.

In a language model: does the non-uniformity help?

Arrange the hidden units of the feed-forward block on a hexagon and let the block convolve two projections of the input across it: y = C·((A·x) ∗ (B·x)). Without wrap-around the output hexagon has twice the radius and is centre-heavy; with wrap-around every output gets the same number of products. This is computed exactly with an FFT, because a hexagon of radius R on a torus is the cyclic group ZN, N = 3R² + 3R + 1, via φ(q, r) = ((R+1)·q − R·r) mod N. The question is not whether it saves operations, but whether the shape gives the model a property it can use.

  • The centre-heavy layer learns an importance order on its own. Trimming the outer rings hurts 2–3× less than dropping random cells (82% of cells kept: +0.08 against +0.23). The uniform, wrapped layer has no such order: outer and random drops cost the same. That gives a ready-made dial for trimming compute at inference, outer rings first. It isn't free (trimming a third costs +0.23), but it's much gentler than trimming at random.
  • More precision at the centre does not help. With the output weights at a 3-bit average: uniform +0.18, more bits at the centre +0.34, more bits at the edge +0.49. Squeezing any ring down to 2 bits costs more than the others gain.
  • Training pushes back against the non-uniformity. Edge activations are about 5× weaker than the centre's, so the edge output weights grow larger and move about 70% further during training. The compensation is partial: the centre still contributes about 3× more, which is why the outer rings are cheaper to trim.
  • Next: train with random outer-ring dropout (Matryoshka-style) to sharpen the order, and use it for elastic width or early exit.

Where this matters: hexagonal grids are standard in geospatial indexing (Uber's H3), board and strategy game maps, telescope and detector arrays, and hexagonal CNNs (HexagDLy). A hexagonal local + skip + random mask is a natural sparse attention for such data. Choosing wrap-around or not decides whether the border learns as fast as the middle.

What the longer runs revealed

  1. Uniform beats every centre/edge weighting. The best triangle is the one with no centre at all (wrapped). Every no-wrap profile ranks below it: full centre, flat or lowered centre. The hexagon said the same thing in section 5: its wrapped single layer also beat the unwrapped one (3.289 against 3.324, seed ranges apart). In both shapes, the version where every output collects the same number of products wins. That is also the property a dense matrix has.
  2. The 1,000-step runs got the ranking wrong twice. There, wrap finished 0.034 behind no wrap, and the reversed triangle looked ahead of the transformer. In the 4,000-step runs wrap pulls ahead from step 800 and the lead grows (0.009 at step 800, 0.031 at step 1,600, about 0.04 from step 2,400 on). The reversed triangle ends 0.044 behind the transformer. Seed spreads also shrank, from 0.19 at the end of a 1,000-step run to at most 0.045 at the end of a 4,000-step run. Short runs with 2 seeds were not enough to rank variants 0.03 apart.
  3. The triangles start fast, fall behind, then close in. At step 400 every triangle except the two centre-lowered ones is slightly ahead of the transformer. By step 1,200 they are 0.07–0.09 behind (0.12 with the centre lowered). From there the gap shrinks steadily as the learning rate anneals: the wrapped triangle goes from 0.073 behind to 0.013. In the last 1,600 steps the triangles improve by 0.16–0.17 bits/char against 0.14 for the transformer, while in steps 400–2,000 the transformer improved more (0.99 against 0.87–0.95). A guess, not tested: the three-way products train less well at a high learning rate, so a lower peak rate or a longer schedule could help them.
  4. The shape is mostly wiring, not weights. The triangle's own weights are only 14,150 numbers, 0.6% of the model (27,900 with wrap). Almost all parameters sit in the projections into and out of the triangle. Yet switching the wiring from "no wrap" to "wrap" moves the loss by 0.04, more than any reweighting of the centre and edges. What matters is which features get multiplied together.
  5. Renormalising made a bad idea consistent. The centre-lowered triangle rescaled to average 1 had by far the tightest seeds (2.787–2.791), while the same profile without rescaling had the widest (2.787–2.832). Rescaling did not make it good, only predictable. This is one observation on 3 seeds.
  6. Per unit of time, the transformer wins easily. Matched on parameters (the triangles even have 3–4% more), the triangle is close to the transformer. But each step is about 4–6× slower on a CPU, so at equal training time the transformer is far ahead. The triangle's case would rest on a fused gather–multiply kernel, which is the same open question as in section 4.

If you try triangle layers: wrap them, leave the weights unscaled, keep all three sweeps in every step and do not expect a fourth direction to help (see below), compare against a dense baseline at 4,000+ steps with 3 or more seeds, and test the learning rate before reading anything into gaps of 0.02. The fair next test of wrap is a smaller wrapped triangle doing the same number of products as the unwrapped one, so that wrap and extra work are separated.

A square with four directions (4 of 4)

The hypothesis: two of three sweeps was worse than three of three, so a fourth direction might do better still, and beat the standard transformer, whose matrices only pair rows with columns.

The layer keeps its weights on an n × n grid that wraps around, and sweeps it at 0°, 45°, 90° and 135°: rows, columns and both diagonals. With n odd, every cell lies on exactly one line of each direction. The first three directions are exactly the wrapped triangle of the sections above, so this is the wrapped triangle plus the second diagonal.

The four line families of the wrapped square. The thick line is a single line: a diagonal that leaves through one edge re-enters at the opposite edge.

Each cell sits on four lines, one per direction. For the output of one of its lines, a cell contributes its weight times the features of its other three lines. Those three can be combined in two ways, and both were trained:

  • One product across all four directions: the three features are multiplied together, so every product ties all four directions together (three give the features, the fourth receives the result). This is the direct extension of the triangle. In this view a plain matrix is the 2-direction case and the triangle the 3-direction case.
  • Two directions at a time: the three features are multiplied in pairs and the pairs are added. This keeps the products two-way, as in the triangle, and changes only the number of directions.

  1. The fourth direction is fully used, but it divides the work instead of adding to it. Switching off any one of the square's four directions costs 0.23–0.25 bits/char, and the added diagonal (0.23) counts about as much as the other three. In the triangle each of the three directions costs 0.40. So each direction matters less in the square: the same job is shared by four directions instead of three, and the final loss is no better.
  2. Removing and adding are not mirror images. Taking a direction away from a model built for three costs 0.10–0.13 even when the model is trained for it. Adding a fourth gains nothing. "Fewer is worse" did not imply "more is better".
  3. At equal parameters, each added direction cost a little. Transformer 2.726, three directions 2.739, four directions 2.754: about 0.014 per added direction, the opposite of the hypothesis. Each single difference is close to the seed noise, and the transformer is not literally the two-direction member of the family (its layers use an activation function instead of products), so read this as a trend, not a law.
  4. Uniform layouts take the top three places. Transformer, wrapped triangle, wrapped square with one product. Every non-wrapped triangle ranks below that square. Whether every output collects the same number of products mattered more than how many directions are swept.
  5. The bolder product trained better. Multiplying three features at once could have been unstable. Instead it beat the cautious two-at-a-time version (2.754 against 2.781), with tighter seeds (spread 0.015 against 0.050).
  6. The fourth sweep was free in time. The square with one product takes 1.05 s per step against 1.14 s for the wrapped triangle, because its smaller grid (41 × 41 instead of 50 × 50 at the first level) more than pays for the extra sweep.
  7. Same learning pattern as the triangles. A fast start (level with the best model at step 400), then 0.07 behind the transformer around step 1,200–1,600, closing to 0.028 at the end.

A square matrix with the usual product, and a rotating offset

A different kind of layer: no sweeps, just ordinary matrix multiplication. Each feed-forward layer turns a token's features into a small square matrix M, computes the usual product M·M, and maps the result back. In the product, the destination cell [x, y] is row y times column x.

In blocks 1–2 the row and the column are taken from neighbours instead, and which neighbours depends on the training iteration. The destination cell itself is not moved:

xoffset = (iteration >> 1) & 1
yoffset = iteration & 1
row = matrix[(y + yoffset) % height]
col = matrix[(x + xoffset) % width]
dest_cell[x, y] = row · col
the row that is used the column that is used the destination cell [x, y]
The offsets cycle through four states, one per training iteration: the cell's own row and column, the next row, the next column, and both. Rows and columns wrap around at the edges.

The matrices are 10 × 10 in blocks 1–2, 11 × 11 in blocks 3–4 and 13 × 13 in blocks 5–6, which gives 2.27M parameters (transformer: 2.25M). Blocks 3–6 use no offset, and validation uses no offset. The first run used a mistyped formula, xoffset = (iteration*2) & 2, which only alternates between two states: no offset, and row y + 1 with column x + 2. It is kept as a second column. A third rule, xoffset = yoffset = iteration & 1, moves the row and the column together: no offset, then row y + 1 with column x + 1. A fourth does the same with a shift of 2: row y + 2 with column x + 2. A fifth cycles through three states: no offset, (x+1, y+1), (x+2, y+2). A staircase instead of straight rows and columns

The offsets above only move the result. This idea changes the product itself. In a matrix product, a destination cell is a sum along its row and along its column, both straight lines. Here both lines become staircases: two cells along, one across, two along, one across. That is a slope of 1 in 2, an angle of 26.565°.

The tilt alternates: +26.5° on even iterations, −26.5° on odd ones. The row path and the column path turn by the same angle, so they stay at right angles to each other. Everything wraps around at the edges.

s = +1 if iteration % 2 == 0 else -1
f(k) = k // 2                      # 2 cells along, 1 across

# through the output cell: the row path is at row y when k == x, the column path at column x when k == y
dest_cell[x, y] = sum over k of
    matrix[(y + s*(f(k) - f(x))) % height][k]  *  matrix[k][(x - s*(f(k) - f(y))) % width]

# from the edge (the first runs): the paths start at row y and column x
dest_cell[x, y] = sum over k of
    matrix[(y + s*f(k)) % height][k]  *  matrix[k][(x - s*f(k)) % width]

Fewer multiplications: a queens mask

The hypothesis: more multiplications did not help, so perhaps fewer do. A regular matrix product multiplies every row with every column. Here only N cells of the N × N matrix take part. This is no longer a matrix multiplication. The question is whether such a cheap operation is good enough as the non-linear step of the layer, the job an activation function normally does.

The N cells are picked as in the eight queens puzzle: one cell in every row and every column, and no two on the same diagonal. A new placement is drawn at random for every layer on every iteration, from a pool of 200 that covers every cell of the matrix, so over time every cell contributes.

How the picked cells are used was open, so both readings were trained. With p[y] the picked column of row y:

  • Mask on both sides (the strict reading): the masked matrix is multiplied by itself. Only N products remain, M[y, p[y]] · M[p[y], p[p[y]]], and only N cells of the result are non-zero.
  • Mask on one side: the masked matrix is multiplied by the full one. Every cell [x, y] gets a single product, M[y, p[y]] · M[p[y], x], so N² products in total.

What this means for the ideas above.

  • The rotating offset and the staircase are not better multiplications. They are ways of making one layer unreliable, and the model is better off without that layer. That is why the exact rule mattered so little, and why every alternating variant landed in the same band.
  • The patterns fit. A larger shift or a steeper tilt changes the output more between states, which pushes the model further from the layer. Paths through the output cell and extra states do the same. The three-state staircase through the cell, the most switched-off variant (0.002), is the best staircase.
  • An exception that matters. With the local product (below), offset models also end up ignoring the layer, yet stay well behind the model without it. Being switched off by the end is not the whole explanation; the layer also costs something along the way.
  • What is not known. This is one seed at 1,000 iterations, in this pyramid model, with this matrix-product layer. It is not known whether the layers stay harmful in a longer run, whether the triangle models would also gain from dropping their first two feed-forward layers, or whether a different learning rate would make the layers useful. The obvious next run is the removed-layer model at 4,000 steps with 3 seeds, next to the transformer.

Letting the model decide: a gain that starts at zero

Instead of removing the feed-forward of blocks 1–2, or disturbing it with an offset, the layer's output is multiplied by one learnable number per layer, a gain, that starts at zero. The layer begins switched off, and training turns it up only as far as it helps. This is the ReZero idea from the published work below. Nine runs, seed 0, 1,000 iterations, same text. The staircase is the two-state one, ±26.5° in turn, with its paths through the output cell. The triangle is the wrapped triangle layer of section 4, sweeping all three directions on every iteration (3 of 3) or two of the three in rotation (2 of 3).

A local product: only the nearby cells

The best model so far has no feed-forward half in blocks 1–2 (their attention half stays) and plain matrix products in blocks 3–6. In a matrix product, every output cell multiplies its whole row with its whole column. Here the cell only uses the cells next to it: about N/3 of them along its row and N/3 along its column, centred on the cell and wrapping around at the edges. That makes it similar to a small cross-shaped convolution.

h = half the window          # window = 2h + 1 ≈ N/3 cells: 3 for N = 10 and 11, 5 for N = 13
dest_cell[x, y] = sum over d in -h..h of
    matrix[y][(x + d) % width]  *  matrix[(y + d) % height][x]
cells used from the row cells used from the column the destination cell [x, y]
First grid: the full product uses the whole row and column of the output cell (10 products per cell). Second: the local product uses the 3 nearest cells of each, including the cell itself (3 products per cell). Third: the local product in the shifted state of an offset rule, with the two arms moved separately. For a 10 × 10 matrix that is 300 multiplications instead of 1,000. Unlike the full product, the local one behaves like a convolution: shifting the matrix shifts the result by the same amount.

Six runs, each the local version of a model above: same seed, same starting weights, same text, 1,000 iterations. With the full product, an offset can be read two ways that come to the same thing. With the local product they differ, so both were run: result shifted moves the whole cross to another cell; arms moved follows the original rule literally, taking the row arm from row y + yoffset and the column arm from column x + xoffset while the output cell stays put (the third grid above).

  • One seed at 1,000 iterations. The local product is a candidate for a cheaper layer, not a result. A window of N/3 was the only size tried.

Few bits: how little precision do these models need?

A model stored and run with very few bits per number is smaller and can run without floating-point hardware. Three measurements on the 1,000-iteration models above, one seed each. The matrix-product feed-forward has a head start: it only multiplies and adds, so on whole numbers it is exact. A standard feed-forward layer needs an activation function (GELU), which is a floating-point function.

Takeaways

The common thread: the rectangle is the attractor. A matrix is a rectangle because a linear map pairs every input with every output. Shapes drawn inside that rectangle drift back towards rectangles when you multiply. Every centred shape becomes its bounding box within 2–3 products. Other shapes stay themselves only when the multiplication changes. Convolution adds shapes instead of composing them, and keeps every convex polygon. And when the polygon describes how matrices are wired together (a ring), what matters is not the shape but its parity.

Surprises

  1. A pentagon can be a stable matrix shape, a hexagon cannot. The more symmetric shape loses. A stable shape is a box cut by at most one diagonal line, and a rectangle cut by one line has at most 5 corners.
  2. Geometry gives the exact cost. Multiplying two P-shaped arrays needs exactly 4|P| − B − 3 multiplications, by Pick's theorem. So the number of lattice points on the edge sets the cost. That's why pentagons, octagons and disks, which can't be regular on a lattice, are about 4% more expensive. The crystallographic restriction shows up as a price.
  3. Odd versus even decides everything for rings. Square, hexagon and octagon rings are incompressible: every fused algorithm needs all 2^k terms. Triangle, pentagon and heptagon rings are compressible. Strassen's speed-up is, in this light, an odd-cycle phenomenon.
  4. Hexagons are made of triangles: a triangular array correlated with its mirror image gives a hexagonal array.
  5. 2D is special. A doubled lattice polygon never has gaps in 2D, but a doubled tetrahedron can (Reeve), so 3D shaped arrays are less tidy.
  6. The natural symmetric generalisation of Strassen doesn't simply appear for the pentagon. A rotation-symmetric 31-term fit froze at error 0.0003. The only lead below 31 is an unconfirmed 30-term approximate decomposition.
  7. The three-way weights tolerate coarse rounding, the projections don't. The cube's 49k three-way weights at 2 bits hurt less than one 33k MLP matrix at 2 bits. The sensitive part is the input projection, whose errors are multiplied together.
  8. Without wrap-around, a hexagon's centre learns about 30× faster than its corners, because more products land there. Wrap-around makes learning uniform.
  9. Hexagonal blurs are exactly round up to 4th order at every step; square blurs only become round slowly.
  10. In a whole model, the triangle beats the hexagon by a wide margin. A pyramid built on hexagonal feature cells with 3 → 1 bottlenecks came close to a standard transformer once its feed-forward was the triangle layer (the wrapped triangle is 0.013 bits/char behind at 4,000 steps, within seed noise), and stayed 0.2 behind with the hexagon's local mixing. The pyramid was never the problem.
  11. The "centre" means different things in the two shapes. In the triangle the long lines are signal (lowering them hurts, raising them is free); in the hexagon the centre-heavy sums were neither harmful nor needed (1/√ balancing changes nothing).
  12. A centre-heavy hexagonal layer learns an importance order without being asked. In a language model, trimming its outer rings hurts 2–3× less than trimming at random. Meanwhile training partly fights the non-uniformity, growing the edge weights to make up for weaker edge activations.
  13. Uniform wins in both shapes. Given the choice between a centre-heavy and a uniform (wrapped) layout, the uniform one won for the hexagonal layer and, in 4,000-step runs, for the triangle feed-forward (2.739 against 2.778 bits/char). No reweighting of the centre and edges beat it.
  14. The shape's own weights are about 1% of the model, yet changing how they are wired (wrap or not) moved the loss more than any centre/edge weighting. The wiring matters more than the weights.
  15. All three sweep directions are needed. Training the triangle with only two of its three sweeps per step, rotating which one is skipped, cost 0.10–0.13 bits/char and saved only about 15% of the step time. Each direction carries information the other two do not.
  16. More directions are not automatically better. A wrapped square swept along rows, columns and both diagonals, at the same parameter count, ended 0.028 bits/char behind the transformer and 0.015 behind the 3-direction wrapped triangle. Dropping a direction hurts, but adding one does not help: the trained square uses all four directions equally, yet each one matters less than in the triangle (0.23–0.25 against 0.40 when switched off). The extra direction divides the work instead of adding to it.
  17. The best trick turned out to be a subtraction. Rotating offsets and staircase paths made a matrix-product model beat the transformer at 1,000 steps. Opening the trained models showed why: they had learned to ignore the layer the tricks were applied to. Removing it outright did better than every trick (3.220 against 3.258, transformer 3.350; one seed). What is removed is the feed-forward half of blocks 1 and 2. Their attention half stays, so the model keeps its six blocks but has four feed-forward layers instead of six, and 17% fewer parameters. The standard transformer does not gain from the same removal (3.378 against 3.350), so the harm is specific to the matrix-product layer.
  18. A model with one-bit weights matched the full-precision transformer. Trained with every weight restricted to ±1, the matrix-product model scores 3.361 against 3.350 for the transformer with 32-bit weights, and the transformer loses twice as much when it is restricted the same way (one seed, 1,000 steps).
  19. Ahead per iteration, behind per second. The best pyramid model beats the standard transformer by 0.16 after 1,000 iterations and 0.07 after 2,000. It needs fewer multiplications per token, yet takes 1.6 times as long per iteration even after optimising the code, because each token carries a vector 3 to 5 times wider. In the same 100 seconds the transformer ends 0.12 better.
  20. Exact search hits a wall quickly. A SAT solver settles the triangle instantly. But it couldn't prove within 15 minutes that the square ring needs more than 15 products, a fact that a one-line flattening argument proves.

The language-model experiments: what stood out

Section 6 grew long. These are its main results, most surprising first. Each says how far it can be trusted: solid means 4,000 steps with 3 seeds; one seed means a single 1,000-step run, usually paired with a control that started from the same weights.

  1. The tricks worked by switching a layer off. Rotating offsets and staircase paths made a matrix-product model beat the transformer. The trained models turned out to ignore the layer the tricks were applied to, and removing that layer did better than any trick (3.220 against 3.258). One seed.
  2. The same layer helps late and hurts early. A matrix product as the feed-forward layer is worth 0.10 in blocks 3–6 against an ordinary layer of the same size, and costs 0.26 in blocks 1–2. Making the early layer wider makes it worse, so it is not a matter of size. One seed.
  3. Asked directly, the model says no. With a learnable gain that starts at zero, the model leaves every blocks 1–2 layer nearly off: queens mask 0.005, staircase 0.01 to 0.05, matrix product 0.03, triangle 0.02 to 0.11. That held at 2,000 steps and for a wide matrix. One seed each.
  4. A model with one-bit weights matched the full-precision transformer. The matrix-product model with every weight restricted to ±1 scores 3.361 against 3.350, and loses half as much as the transformer does under the same restriction. Its feed-forward needs no activation function, so on whole numbers it is exact. One seed.
  5. Ahead per iteration, behind per second. The best pyramid model leads the transformer by 0.16 after 1,000 iterations and 0.07 after 2,000. It needs fewer multiplications per token (1.77M against 2.31M), but each token carries a vector 3 to 5 times wider, and that makes an iteration 1.6 times as long even after optimising the code. In the same 100 seconds the transformer ends 0.12 better. One seed.
  6. A third of the multiplications cost nothing; a random tenth cost everything. A local product that uses only the cells near the output cell matched the full product. A queens mask that picked N cells at random every iteration was the worst of the 1,000-step models. What matters is that the layer computes the same thing every time, not how much it computes. One seed.
  7. Ending up ignoring a layer is not the same as never having it. With the local product, offset models switched the layer off and still finished 0.13 to 0.17 behind the model without it. One seed.
  8. How much a broken layer hurts depends on how loud it is. Skipping one of the triangle's three sweeps cost 0.10 to 0.13 at full strength in every block (solid), and nothing at all behind a gain of a few percent (one seed).
  9. Uniform beats centred. For the triangle and the hexagon, the wrapped layout in which every output collects the same number of products came out best. No reweighting of centre against edge beat it. Solid for the triangle; the hexagon was tested only in a tiny model.
  10. More directions did not help. A square swept along rows, columns and both diagonals ended behind the transformer and no better than the three-direction triangle. The fourth direction was fully used, but it divided the work instead of adding to it. Solid.
  11. Short runs mislead, and the noise is now measured. Rankings from 1,000 steps and 2 seeds were wrong twice at 4,000 steps. Models that are identical in effect differ by 0.015 to 0.04 between single runs, so a gap below about 0.05 means nothing.
  12. What survived the longest test. At 4,000 steps with 3 seeds the standard transformer beat every shaped layer that was run at that length. The best models of the later experiments have not been run that long.

Where this is useful

  • Attention masks are shapes. A causal mask is a triangle, which is closed, so stacking causal layers stays causal. A sliding-window causal mask is a band cut by the triangle. It is not closed: each product widens the band. That widening is exactly how a stack of sliding-window layers gets a receptive field of about (layers × window). Pick these two masks in section 1 to see it.
  • Storage formats that stay closed: triangles, trapezoids and the triangle-in-a-window pentagon can be multiplied without the result leaving the format. Bands and centred shapes need re-allocation after every product.
  • Choosing an array shape for convolution data: hexagons for round, isotropic data (images, sensors, game maps; 13.4% fewer samples); triangles for polynomials of bounded total degree. The cost formula 4|P| − B − 3 lets you budget the work exactly.
  • Mixed-precision storage of shaped layers: keep the small input projection precise and store the three-way weights at 3 bits. In the tiny model the cube then beat the gated block at the same storage (3.530 vs 3.602 bits/char at about 200k bits).
  • Hexagonal data (geospatial H3 cells, game maps, detector arrays): a ring-1 + skip + random sparse mask connects a radius-8 map in 5 layers at 9.5 links per cell, against 16 layers for neighbours only. Wrap-around or not is a design choice: uniform learning, or a centre-first curriculum.
  • Elastic width for free-ish: a non-wrapped hexagonal feed-forward layer orders its own outputs by importance, so outer rings can be trimmed at inference when compute is short, at a fraction of the cost of trimming at random.
  • Testing a new layer shape: 1,000-step runs with 2 seeds ranked these variants wrongly twice (seed spread up to 0.19). At 4,000 steps with 3 seeds the spread fell to at most 0.045 and the rankings held. Budget for that before trusting a gap of a few hundredths.
  • Small, float-light language models: a feed-forward layer built from a matrix product needs no activation function, so with whole-number inputs and −1/0/+1 weights it is exact integer arithmetic, and these models lost half as much as the transformer when trained with one-bit weights. Normalisation and softmax would still need integer replacements.
  • Tensor networks with loops: an even loop can't be shortcut by any fused algorithm, so the order of pairwise contractions is all that matters. Odd loops leave room for Strassen-like tricks.
  • Shaped weights in a language model (triangle, cube; the tetrahedron was too slow to judge) train fine and beat a plain MLP, but not SwiGLU. The cube did best. Their selling point would be 6 multiplications per weight read for memory-bound inference, which is untested without a GPU kernel. The triangles that already pay off in large models live over tokens (2-simplicial attention) or residue pairs (AlphaFold).

How new is this?

Most of it is known in some field: triangular matrices, receptive fields, Newton polytopes and Minkowski sums, Pick's theorem, hexagonal sampling, and Christandl–Zuiddam's cycle tensors. What this page adds is the side-by-side view, the classification of stable matrix shapes (at most 5 corners), the closed cost formula 4|P| − B − 3 for shaped convolution, and the unconfirmed hint that the pentagon ring might be approximated with 30 terms. Also new are the small experiments: shaped weight layers swept three or four ways; the finding that their three-way weights are robust to low precision while the input projection is not; the hexagonal wrap / no-wrap learning-speed contrast; the emergent importance order of a centre-heavy hexagonal layer; and a hexagonal pyramid language model whose wrapped-triangle feed-forward lands within seed noise of a parameter-matched transformer. The isotropy of hexagonal blurs and the power of random long-range links (BigBird, small-world graphs) are known.

How the language-model findings compare with published work

On raw performance there is no comparison. Published transformers reach just under 1 bit per character on the standard enwik8 benchmark (0.97 for the Compressive Transformer with 277M parameters, 0.98 for the Hourglass model). The models in section 6 have about 2M parameters, train for 1,000 to 4,000 steps on 2.5 MB of text and score 2.7 to 3.5. Nothing here competes with the state of the art. The findings are about how small models behave relative to each other.

Feed-forward layers are highly redundant: removing them from the decoder and sharing one across the encoder costs little in translation (Pires et al. 2023). A local product, using only the cells near the output cell, is about as good as the full row and column. Locality is the premise of convolutions. What is less standard. These four do not appear in the papers above. A wider literature search was not done, and each rests on one seed and 1,000 iterations.

  • A clean case of an improvement that was really a removal. The offsets and staircases looked like better multiplications. Switching the layer off in the trained models, and then removing it, showed they worked only by disabling it. Running that control is ordinary good practice; having so clear-cut an example is less common.
  • Ending up ignoring a layer is not the same as never having it. With the local product, the offset models switched the layer off and still ended 0.13 to 0.17 behind the model without it.
  • The same layer helps late and hurts early. Removing the matrix product from blocks 1–2 helps by 0.26, and the same removal costs the transformer 0.03. In blocks 3–6 the matrix product beats an ordinary layer of the same size by 0.10. Published comparisons of feed-forward variants usually swap every layer at once, so a dependence on position would not show.
  • A one-bit matrix-product model matched a full-precision transformer at the same parameter count and number of steps (3.361 against 3.350), and lost half as much as the transformer did under the same restriction.

A mundane explanation, tested and ruled out. In blocks 1–2 the matrix-product layer squeezes 938 features into a 10 × 10 matrix, which is 100 values, about a ninth of the width. The harm could have come from that squeeze. It does not: a 30 × 30 matrix, which squeezes nothing, is just as unwanted and does more harm at full strength (section 6, “Where the matrix product helps, and where it hurts”).

What current practice would suggest trying. Give the layer a learnable gain that starts at zero, so the model switches it on only if it helps. That is ReZero (Bachlechner et al. 2020); LayerScale is the per-channel version (Touvron et al. 2021). It is the principled form of what the offsets did by accident. It was run in “Letting the model decide” in section 6: the gains stay near zero (about 0.03 for the full matrix product, 0.001 to 0.005 for the queens mask), so the model keeps the layer almost off.

So can square matrices be replaced by n-gons?

  • As general linear maps, no. A linear map pairs every input with every output, and that table of pairs is a rectangle. Any other shape is a restriction of it. Only restrictions with at most 5 corners stay closed under multiplication, and the useful one is the triangle, which numerical linear algebra already exploits everywhere.
  • As data with their own multiplication, yes. For convolution-type products (polynomials, filters, images, hex grids), triangles and hexagons are as natural as squares: the product keeps the shape, and the cost is exactly 4|P| − B − 3. Hexagons are the better choice for round, isotropic data; triangles for polynomials of bounded total degree.
  • As the multiplication pattern, odd polygons are where the shortcuts live. Every even ring is incompressible. Every odd ring inherits a speed-up from Strassen's triangle, and the pentagon's exact cost is still open.
