How electric motors work

A wire carrying current in a magnetic field is pushed sideways. Every electric motor is a way of arranging that push around a shaft and keeping it pointing the same way as the shaft turns. This page has six benches, from a single loop of wire to the motors in drills, drones, pumps and printers. Each one is a running simulation you can load, stall, rewire and grab by hand.

One force

A length L of wire carrying current I across a field B feels a force F = B·I·L, at right angles to both. Wrap the wire into coils around a shaft and the force becomes torque. Everything else is bookkeeping.

Every motor is a generator

A coil moving through a field has a voltage induced in it that opposes the supply: the back-EMF. It grows with speed, so the current falls as the motor speeds up. That is why a motor draws most current when stalled, and why it settles at a speed instead of accelerating forever.

Torque is current, speed is voltage

The same constant ties torque to current and back-EMF to speed. Push harder on the shaft and the motor slows a little, the back-EMF drops, more current flows, and the torque rises to match. No controller is involved; it is built into the physics.

Why the current must switch

A coil in a steady field is pushed only until it lines up with the field, then it is held there. To keep turning, either the coil's current or the field must change in step with the rotor. A commutator, a set of transistors, or the alternating mains can each do it.

A field that turns without moving parts

Three coils set 120° apart, fed with three currents 120° apart in time, add up to a field of constant strength that rotates once per cycle. Tesla's idea needs no contacts to the rotor at all, and it is why power grids are three-phase.

Where the losses go

Copper resistance turns current into heat (I²R), bearings and air drag take a share, and iron is heated by the changing field. A small toy motor may turn half its input into work; a large industrial motor more than 95 %.

What is real here

Modelled

  • Force on each conductor from F = B·I·L, with the torque following the coil geometry and the commutator's switching points.
  • Electrical circuits with resistance, inductance and back-EMF, stepped at 10 to 100 microseconds. PWM is switched, not averaged.
  • Field saturation in the shunt and series motors, so the series motor runs on AC and runs away without load.
  • Brushless motor: all three phase currents with a floating star point; in six-step drive the switched-off phase freewheels through its diode until its current reaches zero.
  • Induction motor: the standard per-phase equivalent circuit (Thévenin form), with forward and backward fields when phases are missing.
  • Synchronous motor: torque from the load angle including the reluctance term, a damper cage, and the swing of the rotor about its operating point.
  • Stepper: two phase currents with inductance and back-EMF, a chopper or plain resistor drive, detent torque, and lost steps.
  • Shaft: inertia, viscous drag, dry friction that can hold the rotor still, and brake, fan or linear loads.

Simplified or left out

  • The drawings are schematic cross-sections. Field arrows and pole colours show direction and relative strength; no field equation is solved for the iron.
  • Iron losses, eddy currents, armature reaction, temperature rise and brush sparking are not modelled.
  • The induction motor's torque comes from the steady-state circuit at each instant, so the electrical transients of switching on are missing, and its numbers with missing phases are approximate.
  • The synchronous motor assumes its drive keeps voltage in proportion to frequency; its current and power factor use the round-rotor formula.
  • The brushless motor's sine drive sets voltages from the rotor angle; it is not a full current-regulating field-oriented controller.
  • The single-loop bench ignores the loop's inductance, and the dashed "ideal" lines on the plots ignore inductance too.
  • Motor sizes and values are typical of small machines, not taken from particular products.
