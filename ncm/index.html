Next Concept Modeling

A small, faithful implementation of NCM (Kumbong et al., 2026) running in your browser: a tokenizer turns 64 characters into levels of codes from coarse to fine, and a transformer predicts each level in one parallel pass. Trained on Frankenstein and Shakespeare. The numerics are Rust compiled to WebAssembly.

What this is

Most language models write one token at a time. Next Concept Modeling writes a whole passage at once, but in rounds: first a very coarse description, then finer and finer ones, and only at the end the actual characters. It has two parts that are trained one after the other.

1. The tokenizer: text into levels of codes

A window of 64 characters is turned into 64 small vectors. The tokenizer then describes those vectors in 12 levels. Level 1 has a single code for the whole window. Each later level has more codes, and the last has one per character. A level only has to describe what the levels before it left unexplained (the residual): it averages the leftover down to its own length, picks the nearest entry of its codebook at each position, stretches that back to full length and adds it to the running picture. During training the decoder is often shown only the first few levels, so early levels are forced to carry as much as they can.

2. The generator: predict the next level, all of it at once

With the tokenizer frozen, a transformer learns to predict the codes of level k from the picture built out of levels 1 to k−1. Every code of a level is predicted in the same forward pass, and positions only attend to their own level. Generating a window of 64 characters therefore takes 11 transformer passes, one per level after the first, instead of one per character. The first level is a single code and is simply drawn according to how often each code occurs in the training text.

Two details matter in training. The inputs are deliberately damaged: some of the earlier codes are swapped for random ones, more at coarse levels than at fine ones, so the model learns to cope with its own imperfect earlier choices. And the output layer is tied to the tokenizer's codebooks: the model proposes a vector and the scores are how close each codebook entry is to it.

  • The structure follows the authors' released code: an embedding-only encoder, a one-block decoder, learned down- and up-sampling for level 1 only, codebooks updated by moving averages, tied output heads, rotary positions restarting at each level.
  • The paper says the corruption rate falls linearly from coarse to fine with a mean of 0.5, but the released code has no training loop, so the exact schedule here is this page's choice: the rate runs from the mean plus two thirds of it at the coarsest level to the mean minus two thirds at the finest. The default mean is 0.15, not 0.5. In our runs at this size a mean of 0.5 left the model barely better than guessing at the middle levels, and 0.15 gave clearly lower held-out loss and more word-like samples.
  • Each level has 16 codes here. With 256 the tokenizer reconstructed just as well, but the codes carried several times more randomness than the text itself and the generator had to model all of it.
  • Unused codebook entries are re-seeded from the data here. At the paper's batch sizes that is not needed.

What the numbers mean, and what to expect

All "held-out" numbers are measured on the last 5 % of the selected training text, which training on that selection never uses. If you switch the text without retraining, that slice may be text the current weights have already seen, so the numbers are only clean for the selection the model was trained on. The shipped checkpoint was trained offline with the same Rust code: its numbers appear above when the page loads.

This is a toy. With about a million weights and characters instead of word pieces, the output is word-shaped and often contains real English words, but it is not coherent prose, and nothing here reproduces the paper's quality or speed results. The page makes no speed comparison; it only counts forward passes.
