How a shredder cuts

The cutting head is two parallel shafts, each a stack of hardened steel discs with gaps between them. The discs of one shaft sit in the gaps of the other, so neighbouring discs pass each other like rows of scissors. The shafts turn towards each other, grip the paper and pull it down. Wherever two discs overlap the sheet is sheared, so the strip width is simply the disc thickness. A comb of stripper fingers between the discs peels the strips off so they do not wrap around the shaft.

A strip-cut machine stops there and gives full-length strips. A cross-cut machine has no second cutter: every disc has a few hooked teeth on its rim, and each tooth punches through the strip as it comes round, chopping it to length. The teeth are offset from disc to disc along a helix, which is why neighbouring particles are staggered and why the motor load stays even. Micro-cut is the same idea with thinner discs and more teeth.

Running shreds through a second time is not something the machine does, but it can be done by hand, and turning strips by 90° before the second pass makes a strip-cut machine produce particles. The "Passes" control does that.

What the paper changes

  • Thickness and grammage set how many sheets the motor can pull through. Card counts as several sheets.
  • Fibres and grain. Paper is a felt of cellulose fibres, mostly lying along the machine direction of the paper mill. A dull blade does not shear the fibres, it pulls them out, leaving a fuzzy fringe. The fringe is wider with long fibres and on edges that cross the grain.
  • Moisture. Wet paper loses most of its strength. It tears instead of cutting, the cross cuts often fail so particles stay joined in chains, and the shreds clump and stick instead of scattering.
  • Laminate. Plastic film is tough and stretchy. It loads the motor like several extra sheets and the teeth often fail to punch through.

Putting it back together

Strips keep whole lines of writing and can be matched edge to edge by hand. Particles must be matched in two directions, and blank particles carry nothing to match. The readouts count how many pieces show any content and give a rule-of-thumb rating. For scale: the 2011 DARPA Shredder Challenge had about 10,000 pieces across five documents, and the winning team needed software, human checking and about a month. "Put it back together" on this page cheats: it uses the position every piece is known to have come from.

What is simplified

  • Motor load and sheet capacity come from a formula fitted to typical rated capacities of office shredders, not from fracture mechanics.
  • The tray is a flat top-down view so that every piece stays visible. Where pieces land is drawn from a distribution; they are not rigid bodies.
  • Fringe widths, failed-cut rates and curl are plausible trends, not measurements.
  • With many sheets at once only as many sheets are drawn as fit a piece budget. Load and bin fill use the full stack.
