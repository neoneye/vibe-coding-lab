Spiders

A spider built from its parts: front body and abdomen, eight legs and how long each pair is, the eyes and how they are arranged, the hair, the fangs, and how it moves. Start from a real spider, or from the harvestman that looks like one, then watch it walk, jump, climb over stones and logs or rear up in a threat pose. Spiders straighten their legs with blood pressure: turn it down and the legs curl in, as a dead spider's do. The camera sits low with a slightly wide, fish-eye lens, as if you were down in the leaf litter with it.

A myth says the cellar spider (or the harvestman) is the most venomous spider but its fangs are too short to bite you. Neither part is true: harvestmen have no venom at all, and cellar spider venom is weak to people.

How spiders move

  • Legs on blood pressure. A spider's legs have muscles to bend them but none to straighten two of the main joints. Instead, muscles in the front body squeeze the blood (haemolymph) into the legs, a few kPa at rest and several times that in a sprint or a jump. That is why dead spiders curl up: the pressure is gone and the bending muscles win. Try Blood pressure in the legs.
  • Four legs at a time. Most spiders walk with alternating sets of four legs: left 1, right 2, left 3 and right 4 step together, then the other four. That always leaves the body standing on four feet, a stable table. The duty factor is the fraction of each stride a foot is on the ground; the readout shows how many feet are down.
  • Seven segments per leg. Coxa, trochanter, femur, patella, tibia, metatarsus and tarsus. The high "knee" of a spider is the joint between femur and patella; the model draws a leg as three long parts plus the coxa.
  • Jumping. Jumping spiders crouch, fix a safety line of silk, and push off mostly with their third and fourth legs, landing on the front ones. Here the take-off speed comes from the push (in multiples of body weight) acting over the distance the legs straighten, and the jump follows a ballistic arc without air drag. A jumping spider at about 5× its weight reaches a few body lengths. Blood pressure helps extend the legs, but a 2018 study of the regal jumping spider found its leg muscles could supply the power of the jump on their own.
  • Stop and go. Many spiders run in bursts. Jumping spiders walk in short hops and stop to turn their whole body towards anything that moves, to look at it with their big front eyes.
  • Eyes. Most spiders have eight eyes in two rows. A jumping spider's two big front eyes are like telephoto lenses; a wolf spider's large rear eyes have a reflective layer that shines in torchlight. Some cave spiders have none.
  • Hair. Spider hairs are sense organs: some feel touch, very fine ones (trichobothria) feel air currents, so a spider senses a fly's wingbeats. Many American tarantulas also flick off barbed hairs from the abdomen in defence.

How the model works

Each leg has a phase in the stepping cycle; one tetrapod is half a cycle from the other, and a small lag runs along each side. A foot on the ground stays planted where it touched down; a swinging foot arcs forward to a new foothold half a step ahead. The body rides above the plane through its planted feet, so it tilts when it climbs. A leg is solved as a femur rising at the knee angle, then two more parts that reach the foot. Low pressure folds the legs in. Jumps are thrown with gravity only. It is kinematic, not a physics simulation of forces and contacts. Sizes are in real millimetres; the scene is scaled to the spider so each one fills the view, and the scale bar shows the real size. Preset speeds are typical; only the house spider's sprint is a measured figure.
