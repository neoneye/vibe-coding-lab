Glass Shattering

A drinking glass hits the floor, seen through a high-speed camera. A stress wave crosses the glass in a few microseconds, cracks race out from the impact at about 1.5 kilometres per second and fork when they are fed more energy than one crack can use, and less than a millisecond after the impact the glass is already in pieces, still standing in place. Then the pieces fly. The same model explains why a glass sometimes breaks in two, sometimes into a mix of big and small pieces, and sometimes into hundreds of tiny ones.

    Why big pieces one day and tiny pieces the next

    It comes down to how much stored energy the glass holds when the first crack starts, and where that energy is.

    • Only just enough to break it. A glass only breaks where the stress beats its strength, and its strength is set by its deepest scratch. If the stress only just gets there, there is energy for one crack and little more. That crack runs from the impact up the wall and across the base, and the glass falls apart in two or three big pieces.
    • Energy to spare. A crack needs a fixed amount of energy for each square millimetre of new surface (the fracture energy, about 7 J/m² for glass). A crack fed more than about three times that cannot use it all by going faster, because there is a speed limit (about half the speed of surface waves, roughly 1.5 km/s in glass). Instead it forks. The forks fork again, and the region close to the impact, where the energy is densest, ends up in splinters while the far side of the glass breaks into a few large shards. That is the usual mixed result.
    • Toughened glass. Many tumblers are tempered: blown with cold air so the skin freezes first and is left squeezed while the core is left stretched. The squeezed skin makes them hard to break, but the stretched core holds energy everywhere in the glass, not just where it hit. Once a crack reaches the core it forks and forks across the whole glass, and it bursts into hundreds of small, blunt pieces, sometimes a moment after the impact.
    • The floor and the landing. A stiff floor stops the glass in tens of microseconds, so the glass has to soak up the energy itself. Carpet takes milliseconds and soaks it up instead. Landing flat on the base spreads the force over a large area; landing on the edge of the base or the rim concentrates it.
    • Thickness. The energy a crack needs grows with the thickness of the glass it cuts through, so thick bases and jars break into fewer, chunkier pieces than thin wine glasses.

    What the high-speed camera shows

    • The stress wave (light ring) leaves the impact at about 5 km/s and crosses a tumbler in some 20 microseconds. It carries the news of the impact; until it arrives, the rest of the glass does not know anything happened.
    • The cracks follow at about 1.5 km/s. They start at the deepest flaw in the most stressed spot, which is almost always where the glass touched the floor. The fracture surface right around that origin is smooth as a mirror; further out it turns misty and then rough, where the crack started to fork. The size of the mirror tells a glass expert how high the stress was.
    • Separation. When the cracks stop, after roughly 0.1 milliseconds, the glass is already in pieces but has hardly moved. The pieces drift apart over the next milliseconds, pushed by the released strain energy and by the momentum of the fall, then tumble, bounce and slide.

    How the model works

    • The glass is a body of revolution with a real wall and base thickness. Its surface is covered by a grid of cells smaller than a millimetre.
    • The impact. The fall energy m g h is shared between the floor and the glass by the floor's stiffness. The peak stress follows from that energy squeezed into the contact region; the strength comes from the deepest scratch (σ = KIc / (1.12 √(π a))), with a Weibull scatter between glasses, plus the skin compression of toughened glass. The energy left over once the glass reaches its strength is spread around the contact point, decaying with the distance along the glass; a small share rings through the whole glass.
    • Cracks are tips that walk across the surface at the terminal crack speed. Each tip draws the stored energy from a disc around it, pays the fracture energy times the local thickness for every step, forks when it is fed more than a threshold, stops when it runs out, at the rim, or when it runs into another crack. Where the energy is dense the disc is small, so the cracks end up roughly 2 Gc t / (η e) apart. Flaws away from the impact can start new cracks once the stress wave has reached them.
    • Pieces are the regions of the grid that the cracks cut apart. Each piece gets a launch velocity from the energy it held, and from then on a rigid-body physics engine (Rapier) moves the pieces, with each piece made of small boxes that follow its curved shape.

    Limits

    • Crack paths are a model of what real cracks do, not a solution of the elastic equations. The energy-to-crack-spacing rule and the fork rule are well established for glass; the numbers are tuned by eye.
    • During the first tenth of a millisecond the glass moves less than half a millimetre, so the picture holds it still while the cracks run.
    • Pieces do not break again when they land. A tiny share of the impact zone breaks finer than the grid; it is drawn as glitter.
    • The glass is empty. Water in it would change the impact a lot.
