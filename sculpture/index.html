Ancient Sculpture

Six famous statues made the way they were made: a moai pecked out of a volcanic slope, Nefertiti's bust of limestone and plaster, Michelangelo's David emerging from a block of marble, the Buddhas of Bamiyan cut into a cliff, a terracotta soldier built up from clay, and the Great Sphinx carved out of the bedrock of Giza. Watch each one go from rough stone to the finished statue, step by step, with the tools of its time, then take the tools and cut into the stone yourself.

    Getting the design onto the stone

    A sculptor cannot put stone back, so every culture needed a way to know where to stop cutting.

    • Egypt: grids and drawings on the block. Egyptian sculptors drew the figure from the front and the side on the faces of the block, over a grid of squares. In the classic canon a standing figure was 18 squares from the soles of the feet to the hairline. They cut inwards from each drawing, drew the lines again on the new surfaces and cut again. Unfinished pieces still carry the red and black lines. Watch the Nefertiti bust and the Sphinx.
    • Rome: measuring points. Roman workshops mass-produced copies of famous Greek statues. They measured points on a plaster cast of the original with calipers and plumb lines from a frame, drilled holes in the new block to the right depth at each point, and carved down to the bottoms of the holes. Around 1800 this became the pointing machine, a frame with an adjustable needle that copies a model almost exactly. None of the six statues here was made that way.
    • Michelangelo: the water level. According to Giorgio Vasari, Michelangelo made a small wax model, laid it in a tub of water and slowly let the water out. The parts that came out of the water first were the parts he cut first, working into the block from the front as if the water level were falling through the marble. His unfinished statues look exactly like that: figures half out of the block, finished at the front and rough at the back. Watch David.
    • Rapa Nui: by eye. No measuring tools are known. The moai follow a strict common style, so the carvers worked from a shared idea of the figure, probably laid out with cords on the rock.
    • Qin China: moulds and parts. The terracotta soldiers were assembled from parts. Heads, hands and some body parts were pressed in moulds, a few basic types for thousands of figures, then finished by hand so that every face is different.

    Stones

    Hardness is on the Mohs scale, where a fingernail is about 2.5, copper about 3, iron and glass 5 to 5.5, and quartz 7. A tool cuts what is softer than it. To work stone harder than their tools, sculptors crushed it with tougher stones and ground it with sand, which is quartz.

    How this page works

    Each statue is modelled from memory of photographs and published measurements, out of a few hundred simple shapes blended together (spheres, ellipsoids, rounded cones and boxes), not from 3D scans. The proportions and the signature features are meant to be faithful, the fine detail is simplified. The shapes are turned into a distance field: for every point near the statue, how far it is from the surface. The picture is drawn by stepping along each ray of light through that field. A stage of the work is a rule applied to the field: the stone that is left is the statue thickened by a margin, cut by a falling plane (the quarry floor, Michelangelo's water level), and the margin shrinks stage by stage until it is zero. Your carving is stored in a second grid of the same size; each blow removes a small shape, a chip, a groove, a flat or a drill hole, sized like the real tool. How much a blow removes depends on the tool's hardness against the stone's. Dates, sizes and numbers are from standard references, rounded; where historians are unsure, the text says so.
