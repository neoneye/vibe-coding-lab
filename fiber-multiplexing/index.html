LIGHT · GLASS · COLOUR

Fibre optic multiplexing

One strand of glass thinner than a hair carries the traffic of a whole city. It does so by sending many signals down the same fibre at once, each on its own colour of light, and sorting them apart again at the far end. This page shows how the light stays in, how the colours are combined and separated, what goes wrong when they are packed too tightly, and the other ways of sharing a fibre.

1 · Keeping light inside glass

A fibre is a glass core surrounded by glass that bends light slightly less (the cladding). Light that meets the boundary at a shallow enough angle cannot get out: it is reflected completely, again and again, for kilometres. Steeper light leaks into the cladding and is gone within centimetres.

Angles in the picture are drawn four times steeper than they are, or the zigzag would not fit. In the single-mode fibre used for long distances the core is only 9 thousandths of a millimetre across, about six wavelengths, and the light travels as one wave guided along the core, not as a bouncing ray. The acceptance angle works out the same.

2 · Many colours, one fibre

Each transmitter is a laser of a slightly different colour, switched on and off billions of times a second: on for a 1, off for a 0. A multiplexer merges the beams into one fibre. Light beams do not disturb each other, so they travel together as one mixed stream. At the far end a demultiplexer sorts the light by colour, and each receiver sees only its own flashes. This is wavelength-division multiplexing, WDM.

3 · Sorting light by colour

The classic demultiplexer is a diffraction grating: a surface ruled with hundreds of fine lines per millimetre. Each line scatters the light, and the scattered waves only add up in a direction that depends on the wavelength. A lens turns that small difference in direction into a difference in position, and a row of fibres picks the colours up. Run backwards, the same device is a multiplexer.

Arrayed waveguide gratingThe usual choice today: a chip with dozens of glass tracks, each a fixed amount longer than the last. The differing delays do the job of the grating lines. One chip the size of a stamp handles 40 or 96 channels.
Thin-film filterA stack of a hundred or more transparent layers that passes one channel and reflects all others. A chain of them peels off one colour after another.
Add-drop multiplexerIn the middle of a route, a node takes out the few colours meant for it and lets the rest pass as light, untouched. Reconfigurable ones (ROADMs) steer each colour with tiny liquid-crystal mirrors, under remote control.

4 · Why infrared, and how far it goes

Glass scatters short wavelengths strongly (the reason the sky is blue) and absorbs long ones, leaving a valley of clarity near 1550 nm where light loses only about 4 % per kilometre. Window glass loses that in a few centimetres. Even so, after 80 km only a few percent is left, so the light is boosted about that often.

The booster is the reason WDM took over. An erbium-doped fibre amplifier is a few metres of fibre containing erbium, pumped by a second laser; it amplifies every colour in the band at once, as light, without knowing or caring what the signals are. Before it, each channel had to be turned into electricity and re-sent at every stop. With it, adding a channel means adding a laser at each end only.

5 · Sharing by taking turns: time-division

Before colours, there was the clock. Four slow streams are interleaved into one stream four times as fast: a bit from A, a bit from B, a bit from C, a bit from D, and round again. The receiver deals them back out. It works only if both ends agree where a round begins.

Each colour on a WDM fibre is itself a time-division stream carrying thousands of separate connections. Real streams include a repeating marker pattern so the receiver can find the start of a round by itself.

6 · Stacking every trick

More bits per flashModern receivers measure the light wave itself, its strength and its timing (phase), by mixing it with a laser of their own. A symbol can then be one of 4, 16 or 64 states instead of on or off.
PolarisationLight waves can vibrate up-down or left-right. The two do not mix, so each colour carries two independent signals. The fibre twists them along the way; the receiver untwists them by calculation.
More coloursThe erbium amplifier covers the C band, about 4.4 THz wide. A second kind covers the L band next to it and doubles the room.
More glassWhen one core is full, the next step is space: fibres with several cores, or cables with thousands of fibres. Each is another complete set of colours.

The estimate assumes the symbol rate is 64 % of the channel spacing and that 22 % of the bits are error-correction and framing. Denser symbols need a cleaner signal and so reach less far: 64-QAM is for links between nearby data centres, QPSK for crossing oceans.

What is simplified

Channel and filter shapes are bell curves; a receiver sees the overlap of the two, adds a little noise, and compares with a fixed threshold. Real filters have flatter tops and steeper sides, so real systems pack channels somewhat tighter than this model allows.
  • Left out entirely: the fibre's own effects on the pulses (colours travelling at slightly different speeds, and channels disturbing each other at high power), which set the limits on real long routes.
  • The glass in part 1 has a core index of 1.4682 and a cladding index of 1.4629, typical of standard single-mode fibre.
  • The loss curve is the usual two-term model (scattering falling with the fourth power of wavelength, infrared absorption rising) plus a water peak at 1383 nm as in older fibre. Modern fibre has almost no water peak.
  • The grating picture exaggerates the fan of colours; the numbers under it are the real ones for the first diffraction order.
