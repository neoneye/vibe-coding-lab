Snakes

A snake built from a few hundred vertebrae, each bending only a little, and the four ways it moves without legs. Slithering and sidewinding here are physics: the body only bends, and friction between the belly scales and the ground decides how fast and which way it goes. A snake on glass gets nowhere; give it grip and it flows. Concertina moves it through a tunnel, creeping moves a heavy python in a straight line, and strike shows a viper, cobra or python striking. The camera sits low with a slightly wide, fish-eye lens.

The common kinds

Each card sets every parameter to that snake. The number of vertebrae is estimated from typical counts of belly and tail scales.

Four ways to move without legs

  • Slithering (lateral undulation). Waves of bending run from head to tail. On rough ground the body pushes sideways against stones and grass stems, and every part follows the head along the same S-shaped track. On flat ground it works because the belly scales grip more sideways than forwards: a 2009 study measured friction of about 0.11 sliding forwards, 0.14 backwards and 0.20 sideways on snake skin, and showed that this difference alone can move a snake. Make the three equal and the snake slips in place, slightly backwards, like a snake on glass. Snakes also lift the most curved parts of the body as they go, putting their weight where the body is straight; in the study's model that made them about 35% faster. Try Lift the curves off the ground.
  • Concertina. In a narrow tunnel or a crevice, or along a branch, the snake folds part of its body into tight bends that brace against the walls. It reaches the front forwards from the braced rear, braces the front, then pulls the rear up. It is slow and tiring, but works where nothing else does.
  • Sidewinding. On loose sand, pushing sideways just makes the sand flow away. A sidewinder lifts its body in a second wave, a quarter of a cycle out of step with the bending, so only two short stretches rest on the sand at any time. They stay put while the rest of the body arcs through the air to the next spot, and the snake moves sideways, leaving separate parallel tracks. Watch from above. Try Lift wave ahead by 0° or 180° and the trick stops working.
  • Creeping (rectilinear). Heavy snakes such as pythons and boas move in a straight line, slowly and almost invisibly: waves of muscle pull the belly skin forwards in steps, so the belly scales grip the ground, while the skeleton slides smoothly inside. Each patch of belly skin rests on the ground between steps.

Vertebrae and scales

A snake has more vertebrae than almost any other animal: from about 120 to over 500, and up to about 600 in the Oenpelli python of northern Australia. Each trunk vertebra carries a pair of ribs, and below it one wide belly scale: count the belly scales of most snakes and you have counted their trunk vertebrae (blind snakes, with small even scales all round, are an exception). Each joint bends only a little, more from side to side than up and down, so the curve of a long snake is the sum of many small bends. The side bend per vertebra limits how tight the waves and coils can be.

How the model works

The body is a chain of vertebrae with set lengths. When slithering and sidewinding, the snake's shape is a wave of bending (and lifting) that runs down the body. At every moment the model finds the overall sliding and turning of the whole body for which the friction forces on all the vertebrae cancel out. This is the quasi-static model of Hu, Nirody, Scott and Shelley (PNAS, 2009), with friction depending on whether each part slides forwards, backwards or sideways. It ignores momentum, so it is right for slow and moderate speeds. Grass and stones are approximated as extra sideways grip. Concertina, creeping and striking are drawn from their sequences rather than solved. Sizes are in real millimetres; the scene is scaled to the snake.
