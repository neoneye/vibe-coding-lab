Four numbers

In the 1960s the palaeontologist David Raup showed that most coiled shells can be described by the rate at which the tube widens (W), how far it sits from the coiling axis (D), how fast it slides down the axis (T), and the shape of its outline. He drew them with one of the first computer plotters, then asked why real shells fill only a small part of the space of possible ones.

A pattern is a diary

A mollusc adds shell only at the lip, and pigment goes in as the shell is made. Whatever the cells along the lip are doing at one moment is frozen as one line. Stripes along the coil are cells that stay on; lines parallel to the lip are all cells switching together; oblique lines are a signal travelling from cell to cell.

Why Rule 30

If each cell decides from its own state and its two neighbours', there are 256 possible rules. Rule 30 makes a disorderly crowd of triangles from the simplest start, very like the textile cone, Conus textile. The snail is not computing Rule 30, but a signal that spreads to neighbours and then shuts itself off behaves much the same way; the activator–inhibitor engine shows that version.

What comes from the literature, and what this page chose

From the literature

  • W, D and T are Raup's definitions, measured about the coiling axis. The page's tests check that the mesh obeys each one.
  • A shell pattern as a space–time record of a one-dimensional process at the lip is Hans Meinhardt's account, and the activator–inhibitor equation is his, as written in his book.
  • The likeness between elementary cellular automata and cone-shell patterns was pointed out by Stephen Wolfram.
  • Stripes that stay put need an inhibitor that spreads much faster than the activator; travelling lines need one that does not spread and decays slowly.

Chosen by this page

  • The outline is a superellipse with an egg and a tilt control. Raup left the shape open.
  • Ribs, cords, knobs, lip flare and wall thickness are simple displacements added on top; they are not part of his model. Whorls may pass through one another.
  • The lip has a fixed number of cells and closes into a ring on the hidden inner side. A real lip is an open strip that gains cells as it grows.
  • “Spontaneous firing” is a random kick that stands in for Meinhardt's pacemaker mechanisms. The reaction presets were tuned by eye, not taken from his published tables.
  • Bands and flames are drawn, not grown. The morphospace regions are a rough sketch after Raup's 1966 figure.
  • A horn is a keratin sheath that grows from its base, not at a lip. It shares only the spiral geometry.
