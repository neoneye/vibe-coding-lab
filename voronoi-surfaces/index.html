How it works

Every site p gets the same surface, z = f(x − p), placed over it. At each point of the plane, the site whose surface is lowest owns that point. The front layer is the plane coloured by that winner. Behind it is the lower envelope, the minimum of all the surfaces. The red creases on the envelope are where two surfaces cross, and they project straight down onto the Voronoi edges.

With Euclidean distance and h(d) = d², every surface is the paraboloid z = |x − p|², and you get the ordinary Voronoi diagram. This is the lifting construction, and it is Figure 10 of Boissonnat, Nielsen and Nock, Bregman Voronoi diagrams (Discrete & Computational Geometry, 2010).

Any increasing h(d) gives the same cells: a cone (d), a quartic (d⁴) or d³ − d² + d. Only the heights change. A profile that turns back down, like the ring (d − 0.4)² or the Mexican hat, changes the diagram: a site can lose the ground under itself, and cells can split into pieces.

Weights lift each surface by its own amount. With d² you get the power diagram, which still has straight edges. With the cone d you get the additively weighted (Apollonius) diagram, whose edges are hyperbolas.

Polynomial surfaces. Every quadratic P gives straight edges, even a saddle, because the difference of two moved copies is linear in x. Elliptic and sheared bowls give a stretched Voronoi diagram, and the linear terms shift the whole diagram. Cubic and quartic terms bend the edges. Odd leading terms and saddles are not bowls: they fall away to −∞, so a distant site can win, and cells may hold no site or no ground at all.

Distances. Manhattan |u|+|v| and Chebyshev max(|u|,|v|) give axis-aligned and 45° edges. Where two sites sit exactly on a diagonal, the tie can cover a whole area. Quasi-Euclidean is the octagonal distance from image-processing distance transforms: max + (√2−1)·min. Minkowski p blends them: p = 1 is Manhattan, p = 2 is Euclidean, and large p approaches Chebyshev.
