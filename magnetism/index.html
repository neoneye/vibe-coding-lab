How magnetism works

Every electron is a tiny magnet. In most materials they pair off and cancel; in a few, neighbouring atoms line their magnets up, and the whole piece becomes one. This page has three tables to work at: one for placing magnets and seeing their fields, one for looking inside the metal as it is heated, and one for a dish of ferrofluid.

Poles come in pairs

Field lines leave a magnet at its north pole and return at its south. Cut a magnet in two and each half has both poles again; nobody has found a north pole on its own. The lines are closed loops that run on through the inside of the magnet.

Currents make fields

A wire carrying current is ringed by a magnetic field. Wind the wire into a coil and the rings add up to the field of a bar magnet, with a north and a south end. Put iron inside and the iron's own atomic magnets join in, making the coil tens to thousands of times stronger.

Hard and soft

A permanent magnet is a “hard” material: once magnetised it stays that way. Iron is “soft”: it is magnetised by a nearby magnet, always so as to be attracted, and forgets when the magnet is removed. That is why either pole of a magnet picks up a nail.

Why neighbours agree

The force that lines up neighbouring atoms is not the pull of one little magnet on the next; that is thousands of times too weak. It is the exchange interaction, a consequence of quantum mechanics and electric repulsion between electrons, and it is strong only in a few elements: iron, cobalt, nickel, gadolinium and some of their compounds.

The Curie point

Above a definite temperature the shaking wins and the material stops being a magnet: 770 °C for iron, 1115 °C for cobalt, 358 °C for nickel, about 20 °C for gadolinium. Cooled again it orders again, but in domains pointing different ways, so a magnet heated past its Curie point comes back unmagnetised.

Other kinds of magnetism

If neighbours prefer to oppose, the order is a chequerboard with no net magnetism: an antiferromagnet. If the two sides of the chequerboard are unequal, some is left over: a ferrimagnet, such as ferrite and magnetite. With no coupling, atoms line up weakly and only while a field is on: a paramagnet. Everything else is very weakly repelled: diamagnetism.

3 · Ferrofluid

A ferrofluid is oil carrying magnetite particles about ten millionths of a millimetre across, each one a single magnetic domain, kept apart by a coating and kept afloat by the jostling of the oil. Bring a magnet up under the dish: the fluid gathers over it and, past a threshold, the surface breaks into spikes. Drag to look round.

Why spikes

A small bump on the surface gathers field lines, as any magnetic point does, and the stronger field at its tip pulls it up further. Gravity and surface tension pull it back down. Below a certain magnetisation they win and the surface stays flat; above it the bumps run away into spikes, spaced about a centimetre apart: the spacing at which gravity and surface tension together resist least.

No memory

Each particle is a permanent magnet, but it is free to turn, and heat keeps turning it. With no field the particles point every way and the fluid is not magnetic at all; in a field they line up more and more until all of them have. Take the field away and the magnetism is gone at once. That behaviour is called superparamagnetism.

Heat

Warm fluid is jostled harder, so the same field lines up fewer particles and the fluid is less magnetic. Engineers use this: fluid near a hot part is less attracted than cool fluid, which is drawn in to replace it, so a magnet can pump coolant with no moving parts. Loudspeakers use ferrofluid round the voice coil for this reason.

What comes from the real thing, and what this page chose

From the real thing

  • The field of a magnet from its pole faces, falling off as the inverse square from each bit of pole; the field of a long wire; like poles repel, unlike attract, and iron is attracted to both.
  • Remanence, Curie points and working limits of the four magnet families are typical manufacturers' figures. Each weakens reversibly as it warms; past its working limit it loses strength for good; at the Curie point it stops being a magnet.
  • The lattice is the Ising model, the standard minimal model of a magnet. Its Curie point and the curve drawn beside it are exact results.
  • Ferrofluid magnetisation follows the Langevin law. The threshold for spikes and their spacing are the Cowley–Rosensweig results. The fluid gathers where the field is strong by the balance of magnetic pressure and weight, conserving its volume.

Chosen by this page

  • The field table is a flat slice through three-dimensional magnets with square pole faces. Each magnet is uniformly magnetised and does not weaken its neighbours; in reality a strong magnet can demagnetise an alnico or ferrite one.
  • Field lines are drawn outside the magnets only. Iron is magnetised along its length only, by a single “response” number. Magnets stay where you put them: forces are shown, not acted on.
  • The shape of the strength-against-temperature curve is one empirical formula (Kuz'min's) with a parameter chosen per material to match its quoted loss per degree. Permanent loss is a smooth ramp from working limit to Curie point; real loss depends on the magnet's shape and what it is attached to.
  • Real atoms' magnets can point any way, not just up or down, and real domains are set by the shape of the piece and its crystal, which the Ising lattice leaves out.
  • The spikes are not a solution of the fluid's equations. Where and how strongly the surface is unstable is computed from the physics; the pattern is then grown by a standard pattern-forming equation (Swift–Hohenberg) tuned to the right spacing, and spike height is that pattern scaled by a fixed fraction of the spacing. Viscosity only sets the speed.
  • The magnet under the dish is treated as recovering fully when cooled, unlike the ones on the field table.
