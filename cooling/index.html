How Cooling Works

What a fridge really does to your food, and four ways to keep food cool where there is no mains electricity: solar power, a pot of wet sand, a kerosene flame, and the night sky.

Heat only flows downhill

Heat always flows by itself from something warmer to something colder: from the hot air into the food, never the other way. Shade and insulation slow that flow down, but they can never make food colder than the air around it. To get food below the air temperature you need a way to send its heat somewhere. This page shows four ways, and the temperature ladder beside each picture shows where the heat goes.

  • Pump the heat uphill with work (the ordinary fridge). A fluid boils at a low temperature inside the box and takes up heat. A compressor squeezes the vapour until it is hotter than the room, and it gives the heat away there.
  • Evaporate water (the zeer pot). Water needs a lot of energy to turn into vapour, 2.4 MJ for each kilogram. When it evaporates from wet clay, that energy comes out of the pot. The limit is the wet-bulb temperature, set by how dry the air is.
  • Pump the heat uphill with heat (the absorption, or kerosene, fridge). A flame boils ammonia out of water. Ammonia that has boiled and soaked back in does the same job as the compressor, with no moving parts.
  • Radiate it to space (the night-sky ice pond, and the daytime radiative panel). On a clear, dry night the sky acts like a surface at −20 °C or colder. Shallow water facing it can freeze even when the air stays above 0 °C. A panel that reflects almost all sunlight can do the same at noon.

Three newer ideas each get a tab of their own: a daytime radiative cooling box, a dew-point evaporative cooler that beats the wet-bulb limit, and a solar adsorption ice maker, where the sun drives a refrigerant out of a bed by day and it soaks back in at night.

How a fridge works

The fridge has a closed loop of refrigerant. Modern home fridges use isobutane (R600a), which boils at −12 °C at normal air pressure.

  1. Evaporator (inside, cold). The refrigerant arrives as a cold liquid at low pressure and boils. Boiling takes heat, so the plate goes cold, colder than the food. Heat flows downhill from the food and air into it.
  2. Compressor (the black dome at the back). It sucks the vapour away, keeping the pressure low so the liquid keeps boiling, and squeezes it. Squeezed vapour gets hot and has a higher boiling point.
  3. Condenser (the black grid on the back, warm). The vapour is now hotter than the kitchen, so it gives its heat to the room air and condenses back to a liquid.
  4. Capillary tube (a long, very thin tube). It holds the high pressure back. As the liquid squeezes through, its pressure falls and some of it flashes to vapour, and that chills the rest down to the evaporator temperature. Then the loop starts again.

A thermostat switches the compressor on when the inside warms past the set point plus half the switching band, and off again below. Every joule taken out of the food, plus every joule the compressor uses, comes out of the condenser. So a fridge always heats the kitchen.

The work needed grows with the height of the "lift" from evaporator to condenser. A hot room, dust on the coils or a fridge pushed against the wall all make the condenser run hotter, so the fridge uses more electricity. If the condenser gets too hot, the compressor's overload switch cuts it out. The ideal (Carnot) number is COP = Tevap / (Tcond − Tevap), in kelvin. Real fridges reach roughly a third to a half of it.

Heat also leaks in through the walls (insulation thickness and material), past the door seal, and every time the door opens. In humid air, a door opening also lets in water vapour, which freezes onto the evaporator as frost and costs energy to remove.

Without the grid: solar fridges

A solar panel can run a small DC compressor. The hard part is the night.

  • With a battery. The battery stores electricity for the night, but batteries wear out and are often the first thing to fail.
  • Solar direct drive (the design used by WHO-approved vaccine fridges). It has no battery. The compressor runs only while the sun shines, and freezes packs of water lining the cabinet (the ice bank). Melting ice soaks up 334 kJ per kilogram at a steady 0 °C, which keeps vaccines between 2 and 8 °C through the night and cloudy spells.

The zeer pot (pot-in-pot cooler)

An unglazed clay pot sits inside a larger one, with wet sand in between and a wet cloth on top. Water seeps through the outer pot and evaporates from its surface. The heat to evaporate it comes mostly from the sand and the inner pot, so they cool down.

The pot-in-pot idea is very old. In the 1990s the Nigerian teacher Mohammed Bah Abba popularised the zeer across northern Nigeria, where it let farmers keep tomatoes and peppers for weeks rather than days.

  • It only works in dry air. Nothing cooled by evaporation can get below the wet-bulb temperature. On the humid coast that is only 2 to 4 degrees below the air; in the dry Sahel it can be 15 degrees below. Try the two "Zeer pot" situations.
  • It needs water every day: a few litres for a pot this size, more in hot, windy weather.
  • Keep it in shade with moving air. Sun on the pot heats it; still air stops the evaporation.
  • Good for vegetables and fruit (it also keeps them humid). It is not cold enough for milk, meat or vaccines.

To build one: take two unglazed clay pots with a gap of a few centimetres between them, put sand in the bottom and the gap, soak the sand, and cover with a damp cloth. Keep it in a shady, airy place and add water once or twice a day. Walk-in "charcoal coolers" in East Africa work the same way, with wet charcoal between two layers of mesh.

The kerosene (absorption) fridge

Baltzar von Platen and Carl Munters invented this fridge in Sweden in the 1920s, and Electrolux built it. It runs on a small flame, so it was the clinic and farm fridge across rural Africa for decades.

It has ammonia, water and hydrogen in one sealed loop, with no pump and no moving parts:

  • The flame heats a boiler, where ammonia boils out of the water. The bubbles lift liquid up a narrow tube like a coffee percolator (the bubble pump).
  • The ammonia vapour condenses in the fins at the top.
  • The liquid ammonia runs into the evaporator, where it evaporates into hydrogen gas and gets cold.
  • In the absorber, water soaks the ammonia back up and the hydrogen returns to the evaporator.

Heat-driven cooling is less efficient than a compressor. A coefficient of performance (COP) of 0.2 to 0.4 means one joule of heat removed for every 3 to 5 joules of flame. It also struggles in very hot rooms, because the fins must still get rid of the heat. The ideal number is COP = (1 − Ta/Tg) · Te/(Ta − Te), where Tg is the boiler, Ta the fins and Te the evaporator, all in kelvin. The same unit can run from a solar heat collector, but then it needs an ice bank for the night.

Ice from the night sky

On clear, dry nights, people in Iran and India made ice in shallow pools and trenches even though the air never froze. In Iran it was kept through the summer in the great domed yakhchāl ice houses, and the Indian ice pits were bedded with straw. The water radiates heat to the sky. With little water vapour in the air, the sky's "temperature" is far below the air's (Berdahl & Martin's clear-sky emissivity, from the dew point).

  • A tall wall shades the pool and keeps the wind off. In very dry air, though, wind also speeds up evaporation, which cools the water, so the wall can cost a little ice. Try it both ways.
  • Straw underneath stops the warm ground from melting the ice.

Clouds or humid air stop it at once. Try "Ice pond on a cloudy night".

Daytime radiative cooling

The night-sky trick also works at noon if the surface hardly absorbs any sunlight. In 2014 a Stanford group showed a multilayer film that sat about 5 °C below the air in direct sun. White paints loaded with barium sulfate now reflect about 98 % of sunlight. The rest of the trick is to shed heat as infrared. Much of it escapes through the atmospheric window, the 8 to 13 µm band where dry air is nearly transparent, so it goes straight out to space.

  • Reflectance matters most. Each per cent of sunlight absorbed is about 10 W/m² at noon, about as much as the whole cooling power. Ordinary white paint (about 85 %) ends up warmer than the air at midday; try the "White paint" situation.
  • Paint or film. A broadband emitter (paint) radiates at every infrared wavelength. It cools hardest near air temperature but stalls sooner. A window-only emitter (film) has less power near air temperature but can go colder.
  • Wind shield. A thin polyethylene film lets infrared out and keeps the warm air off the panel.
  • Thermosiphon. Cold liquid sinks from the panel into the box, so heat only flows from the box up to the panel, never back down. A cold store of water packs, chilled at night, carries the cold through the day.
  • Humid air and clouds close the window, just as for the ice pond. Try "Radiative box on the humid coast".

Dew-point evaporative cooling

A zeer pot or a swamp cooler can only reach the wet-bulb temperature, and it makes the air damp. An indirect cooler keeps the delivered air in dry channels and evaporates water in the channels next to them, so the air is cooled through a thin wall without getting wet. The dew-point cooler (the Maisotsenko cycle) adds one more step. Part of the already-cooled dry air is turned back through the wet channels. Because that air is already cooler, it can take the dry side below the wet bulb, toward the dew point. The price is more water and a fan, which a small solar panel can run.

The solar adsorption ice maker

Some solids soak up vapour: activated carbon soaks up methanol, and silica gel soaks up water. Cool, they hold a lot; hot, they let it go. The ice maker uses this with one daily cycle and no moving parts:

  • Day: the sun heats the bed in a glazed collector to about 100 °C. The methanol is driven out, condenses, and collects as liquid in the evaporator, which sits in the cold box.
  • Night: vents open under the collector and the bed cools. It starts soaking the vapour back up, which keeps the pressure very low. The liquid in the evaporator boils at −5 to −10 °C and freezes the water around it.

Machines like this, studied in the 1980s by Pons and Guilleminot, make roughly 4 to 6 kg of ice per square metre of collector a day. That is a solar COP of about 0.1. Silica gel and water is safer, but the evaporator cannot go below 0 °C, so it makes chilled water rather than ice.

What is simplified

  • Lumped thermal masses. The food, the inside air, the ice bank, the wet sand and the pond are each one temperature. Heat flows between them through conductances worked out from the geometry and typical surface coefficients.
  • The compressor cycle is a fixed fraction of Carnot between the evaporator and condenser temperatures, which are solved from the heat flows. Refrigerant pressures come from a two-point fit of each fluid's boiling curve, accurate to a few percent. Real compressors change capacity with suction pressure; this one has fixed power.
  • The absorption unit is a fixed fraction of the ideal heat-driven cycle, with a boiler that must warm up first.
  • The radiative panel uses a two-band sky: 32 % of thermal radiation lies in the 8–13 µm window, whose emissivity is derived from the Berdahl–Martin total. The panel loses heat by radiation and gains it from sunlight, the air and dew.
  • The evaporative coolers use an exchanger effectiveness of 1 − exp(−NTU). The dew-point cooler's output is measured against the dew point; the others' against the wet bulb.
  • The adsorption bed uses the Dubinin–Astakhov equation for carbon and methanol (Critoph's AC-35 constants) and a Freundlich-type fit for silica gel and water. The uptake approaches equilibrium at a fixed rate, and check valves to the condenser and evaporator switch the cycle.
  • Evaporation and humidity. Evaporation uses the Lewis analogy (mass transfer follows heat transfer), with saturation pressures from Buck's equation. The wet-bulb temperature is solved from the psychrometric equation.
  • Climates are rounded, typical figures for the season named, with a smooth day curve and a constant dew point. They are not weather records.
  • Shelf life is a rule of thumb: the "Q10" rule, where every 10 °C warmer speeds up spoiling 2.5 to 3 times. The ideal ranges are common storage guidance. Real food varies a lot.
