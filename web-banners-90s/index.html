How 90s banners worked

Sizes

In 1996 the Internet Advertising Bureau fixed the sizes: the 468×60 full banner, the 234×60 half banner, 120×90 and 120×60 buttons, the 125×125 square, the 120×240 vertical and the 88×31 micro button that every homepage put at the bottom ("Best viewed with…"). The 728×90 leaderboard and the 160×600 skyscraper came at the end of the decade. Banners here are drawn at those pixel sizes and only ever scaled by whole numbers, so a pixel stays a square.

256 colours

Most screens ran in 256-colour mode, and an animated banner was a GIF, which holds at most 256 colours. Browsers on such screens forced pictures into the 216 "web-safe" colours (six levels each of red, green and blue), which is why so many sites used #3366CC and #FFCC00. The Mac's own system palette added ramps of reds, greens, blues and greys. An artist who wanted smooth gradients built an adaptive palette per image instead, with fewer colours to keep the file small.

Dithering and banding

With few colours a gradient turns into stripes (banding). Dithering trades the stripes for a pattern: ordered dithering adds a fixed 8×8 Bayer threshold grid (a regular cross-hatch), and error diffusion (Floyd–Steinberg) pushes each pixel's rounding error onto its neighbours, giving grain. In an animated GIF the diffusion grain changes from frame to frame, so dithered areas shimmer.

GIF animation

A GIF animation is a list of frames, each with a delay in hundredths of a second, and a "loop forever" block that Netscape 2.0 introduced. Banner makers held a frame for a second or more and flicked through short transitions in between. Ad networks capped the file size, commonly around 12 KB, which is why banners had few frames, few colours and lots of flat areas (GIF's LZW compression loves runs of one colour). Saving a GIF here reports its size against that cap.

JPEG artifacts

JPEG splits the picture into 8×8 blocks, turns each into frequencies (a discrete cosine transform), rounds the frequencies coarsely, and stores colour at half resolution. Low quality shows as blocks, ringing next to sharp edges and colour bleeding. Pictures that are screenshotted and re-saved again and again pile the damage up. The JPEG setting runs that whole round trip in the page, block grid and all, with each re-save shifted like a re-cropped screenshot.

Text

Photoshop smoothed text edges against the background colour; MS Paint did not. The two hand-drawn bitmap fonts (5×7 and 3×5) are for LED signs, pagers, terminals and micro buttons. The other fonts are the web-safe ones every Windows and Mac machine had, and the page uses your system's copies.
