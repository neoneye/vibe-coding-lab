How radiation works

Where it comes from, what stops it, how a Geiger counter turns it into clicks, what a sievert measures, and what you are actually exposed to in a basement, on a city street and in an aeroplane.

1 · What radiation is

Radiation is energy travelling through space, either as waves (light, radio, X-rays) or as fast particles. Most of it is harmless: radio, Wi-Fi, heat and visible light do not carry enough energy per packet to damage a molecule. The kind people worry about is ionising radiation: each packet carries enough energy to knock an electron off an atom, which can break chemical bonds, including those in DNA.

Blue: non-ionising. Red: ionising. The boundary runs through the ultraviolet.

Most ionising radiation comes from atoms whose nucleus is unstable. Sooner or later such a nucleus rearranges itself and throws out the surplus energy. Nobody can say when one particular atom will do it; all that is fixed is the half-life, the time after which half of any large collection has decayed.

Each dot is one atom. Every atom has the same chance of decaying in the next moment, however old it is. The white curve is the half-life law; the yellow line is what these 480 atoms did. Here one half-life takes four seconds whatever the atom; only the label on the clock changes.

2 · Four kinds, and what stops them

α alpha: two protons and two neutrons. Heavy and slow, stops in a few centimetres of air or a sheet of paper. β beta: a fast electron. Gets through paper, stops in a few millimetres of metal. γ gamma: a packet of light. Never stopped completely, only thinned out; dense material works best. n neutron: no charge, passes through lead. Slowed by light atoms such as the hydrogen in water.

The percentages on the right count what gets through. Alpha is the most damaging kind once it is inside the body and the most harmless outside it, because it cannot get through the dead outer layer of skin.

3 · How a Geiger counter works

The heart of a Geiger counter is a sealed metal tube filled with thin gas, with a wire down the middle held at about 400 volts. Nothing flows, because gas is an insulator. Then a particle comes through.

  1. A particle enters through the thin window or the wall and knocks electrons off a few gas atoms.
  2. The freed electrons rush towards the positive wire. Close to it the field is so strong that each one knocks more electrons loose, and those do the same: an avalanche that spreads along the whole wire.
  3. For a few millionths of a second the gas conducts. That pulse of current is the click.
  4. The heavy positive ions drift slowly outwards. Until they are gone, for about a ten-thousandth of a second, the tube cannot fire again. This dead time is why a Geiger counter under-reads in a very strong field.

Every pulse is the same size, whatever caused it. A Geiger tube therefore counts events but cannot tell what kind of particle it was or how much energy it had. The dose rate on the display is the count rate multiplied by a factor that is only right for the gamma rays the instrument was calibrated with, usually caesium-137. The clicks come at random, so short readings jump about: 100 counts are only good to about ±10 %.

4 · Becquerel, gray and sievert

becquerel (Bq)How active a source is: one becquerel is one decay per second. It says nothing about harm. Your own body is about 8000 Bq, mostly potassium-40 and carbon-14.
gray (Gy)How much energy tissue absorbed: one gray is one joule per kilogram. A physical quantity you could in principle measure with a thermometer.
sievert (Sv)How much harm to expect. It starts from the gray and weights it by how damaging the kind of radiation is and how sensitive the exposed organ is. A sievert is a large dose; everyday doses are thousandths (mSv) or millionths (µSv).

Alpha particles dump all their energy along a very short, dense track, so the same absorbed energy does about twenty times the biological damage of gamma rays. And the same dose to the lungs matters more than to the skin. The effective dose in sieverts folds both into one number, so that an X-ray of a tooth, a year of radon and a long flight can be compared.

For small doses the sievert is a measure of risk, not of injury: roughly a 5 % added lifetime chance of fatal cancer per sievert, spread over a population. Below about 100 mSv the effect is too small to measure directly and that figure is an extrapolation. Large doses received within hours are different: they kill cells outright and cause radiation sickness.

Averaged over the world, natural background gives a person about 2.4 mSv a year: roughly half from radon, a fifth from the ground and buildings, a sixth from cosmic rays and an eighth from the food and water that became your body. Medical imaging adds about 0.6 mSv on average, far more in countries that use many CT scans. Everything else people have added, including fallout from weapons tests and Chernobyl and the routine operation of nuclear power plants, comes to less than 0.01 mSv a year for the average person.

What is simplified, and where the numbers come from

  • The place figures are typical values, built from the world averages of UNSCEAR's 2008 report (cosmic 0.39, ground and buildings 0.48, breathed in 1.26, eaten and drunk 0.29 mSv a year). Real places vary several-fold with geology, building materials and ventilation. The basement's gamma and cosmic values are estimates.
  • Radon dose uses UNSCEAR's conversion (9 nSv per Bq·h/m³ of decay products, with the decay products at 40 % of equilibrium indoors and 60 % outdoors). The newer ICRP coefficients give about twice as much.
  • The cosmic-ray dose rate with altitude is a single exponential fitted to typical published values (about 0.035 µSv/h at sea level, 3 to 6 µSv/h at cruising height). It ignores the solar cycle and solar storms, and the latitude setting is a rough factor.
  • Shielding numbers are for one energy each (caesium-137 gamma rays at 662 keV, neutrons of about 1 MeV) in a narrow beam. Scattered radiation, which matters in thick shields, is left out.
  • The Geiger tube is modelled on a common small tube: about 175 counts per minute per µSv/h of caesium-137 gamma rays, and 190 µs dead time. Source strengths are illustrative. The avalanche is drawn about a hundred thousand times slower than it happens.
  • Radiation and tissue weighting factors are those of ICRP Publication 103 (2007). Doses on the chart are typical figures; medical doses vary by a factor of several between machines and patients.
