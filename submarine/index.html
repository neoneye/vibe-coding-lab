Submarine

A submarine dives by letting sea water into its ballast tanks, holds its depth with small tanks and its planes, and surfaces by blowing the water out with compressed air. Fly one from the side, then from the captain's seat.

How it works ▾

  • Weight against displaced water. A boat floats when it weighs less than the water its hull pushes aside. A submarine on the surface has its main ballast tanks full of air; the hull volume of those tanks is its reserve buoyancy, typically a tenth of the displacement.
  • Diving is opening the vents. The main ballast tanks are open at the bottom. Open the vents at the top, the air escapes and the sea floods in. With the tanks full the boat is close to neutral: it neither floats nor sinks.
  • Surfacing is blowing. High-pressure air pushes the water out through the flood holes. Deep down the air is squeezed, so each kilogram of air pushes out less water. On the way up the same air expands (Boyle's law), the boat gets lighter and rises faster. Watch the air in the cutaway grow as the boat climbs.
  • Neutral is a knife edge. The hull is squeezed a little more than the water around it, so the deeper the boat goes the heavier it gets, and a heavy boat goes deeper. Below a thermocline the water is colder and denser, so the boat is suddenly lighter there. Trimming is never finished.
  • Fine weight and balance. The compensating tank pumps water to or from the sea; pumping out against deep water pressure is slow. The trim tanks move water fore and aft to level the boat.
  • Planes need speed. Hydroplanes are small wings: lift grows with speed squared and is zero when stopped. A boat moving a few knots holds depth with its planes even when it is a tonne or two out of trim. Stern planes set the angle of the whole hull, bow planes push the bow up or down. "Rise on the stern planes" pushes the stern down so the nose comes up.
  • Stability like a pendulum. The centre of gravity sits below the centre of buoyancy. Tilt the boat and the pair pulls it back level. On the surface a round hull has its metacentre at the hull axis, so the same rule applies.
  • Deep craft work differently. A research submersible carries steel weights: it sinks with them aboard and drops them to stop and to come up. A bathyscaphe hangs under a float full of gasoline, lighter than water, and drops iron shot to slow down and to rise. Gasoline compresses more than water, so the bathyscaphe gets heavier as it goes down.

What is simplified ▾

  • The hull is a rigid body of revolution cut into 14 stations. Each gives buoyancy, cross-flow drag and contact with the seabed. There is no free-surface effect in the tanks and no wave pattern made by the boat.
  • Water in a main ballast tank finds its level at once; only the vents and the blow valves limit the flow. Air temperature is constant, the bank empties by choked orifice flow.
  • Planes, fins, rudder and sail are thin wings with a simple stall. The hull's own lift is a Munk moment and cross-flow drag; real boats have measured hydrodynamic coefficients.
  • Sea water uses a linear equation of state (temperature, salinity, pressure). The thermocline is a smooth step.
  • The engine is a power limit; diesel power only on the surface, electric power submerged. No battery, no snorkel.
  • Preset figures (sizes, powers, depths, tanks) are approximate and typed from memory; they are there to give each boat its character, not to document a class.
  • The outside view sees further than the water allows, so the boat stays visible from a distance.
  • A real attack submarine has no window. The 3D map's forward view stands for a camera in the bow, or a submersible's viewport; the instruments are what a captain would really fly by.
  • The hull collapses at the collapse depth exactly. Real hulls have scatter, fatigue and a margin over the test depth that varies by design.
